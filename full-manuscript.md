## Lost Introduction

>Once, the fire of heaven burned bright,
A temple crowned in holy light.
His voice like thunder shook the place,
A trembling people, face to face.

>But whispers crept, and hearts grew cold,  
The truth was bartered, bought, and sold.  
Tradition veiled the sacred way.  
The flame grew dim, then died away.

>The priests still knelt, the choirs still sang,  
But hollow were the hymns that rang.
The altar stood, the rites went on,
Yet all the glory gone, withdrawn.

>Yet buried deep in dust and stone,
A Word remained though long unknown.
A scroll once lost, now speaks today:
Return, dear Church, to Yahweh’s way.

---

>“My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge. Because you have rejected knowledge, I also will reject you from being priest for Me; because you have forgotten the law of your God, I also will forget your children.” (Hosea 4:6)

---

> “We must have a new reformation. There must come a violent break with that irresponsible, amusement-mad, paganized pseudo-religion which passes today for the faith of Christ.” - A.W. Tozer[^1]

---

Long ago, God’s people worshiped in the fire of His presence and lived with a burning devotion to Him. The Temple in Jerusalem stood in unmatched splendor. Its golden sanctuary glowed with holy light; the inner chamber held the very presence of Yahweh. At the dedication, fire fell from heaven, and the atmosphere was so electrifying that the priests couldn’t stand to minister.[^2] Yet, somehow, they lost it all.

By the time of King Josiah, that sacred house had become defiled. For decades, the rhythms remained, the Temple still stood, but the glory of God had departed. And the most disturbing fact?

No one even noticed.

Slowly, the holy of holies became a site of unspeakable compromise, idols erected, children burned alive; an entire generation raised worshipping the greatest of abominations, all the while still claiming to follow Yahweh. 
 
And then, one day, a lost Book was found.

During Temple restorations, the high priest uncovered the abandoned Book of the Law. And as this Word was laid open, Josiah’s heart was laid bare. He tore his robe. He lamented with tearful passion:

>“Great is the wrath of the Lord that is aroused against us, because our fathers have not obeyed the words of this book...” (2 Kings 22:13)

The Word of God had been buried under generations of compromise and complacency. He realized they had been living a lie. They still bore Yahweh’s name, but their lives bore no resemblance. Josiah faced a choice: cling to the comfortable status quo, or revive the forsaken truth.

Josiah chose revival.

He tore down pagan altars, cast out false priests, and led the people back to God and His Commandments. His actions, though unpopular, saved the remnant of Israel.

### The Way We’ve Lost

We live in a time chillingly similar to Josiah’s. The structures of faith remain. Sermons still echo. Songs still rise. But something is missing. Have you felt it? That hollow ache, that nagging sense that the lifeblood has gone out. Like a recipe without its key ingredient.

How did the Church, once so pure and beautifully different from the world, become the fractured and often corrupted institution we see today? Much of Christianity today is missing what I call the Mark of God. And it has forgotten what I’ve identified as the seven foundations of the early Church. 

This book seeks to answer the question: _How did Christianity become Lost Christianity?_

It unfolds in two parts:

**Part One: The Two Marks**: This part traces the biblical foundation of God’s Mark on His people, from Passover to Sabbath, from the Shema to the seal in Revelation. You’ll discover that the Mark of God isn’t abstract theology, it’s righteousness and relationship lived daily. And we will contrast this with the infamous Mark of the Beast.

**Part Two: The Two Churches**: This part exposes how the Christian faith has drifted from its apostolic roots into ritual and religion. It identifies seven lost foundations and draws a practical path back to reclaim them.

Whether you’re a burnt-out church leader, a disillusioned disciple, or just waking up to the cracks in the foundation, we need to talk.

We may assume the faith we know today has always been so, but history tells a different story. Early Christianity was defined by simplicity, intimacy, and power, but like Josiah’s day, we have inherited a faith that has drifted. The story of King Josiah recovering the lost Book of the Law serves as a mirror to our time. And it’s time for us all to decide.

Will we stand with the likes of King Josiah? 

Because it’s time to renovate the Temple, only this time, the Temple is us.

# Part One: The Two Marks

## Chapter 1: The Mark of God - Branded in Brokenness

>Two paths diverge, one dark, one light.  
>Two Marks divide, one death, one life.

---

>“If you love Me, keep My commandments” (John 14:15).

---

I love Jesus, but I haven’t always been devoted to Him.

I have been the disciple, so full of hope and fire that I offended people with my love for God. Literally. 

But I’ve also been the one caught in the machinery, obsessed with the optics, the smoke and lights designed to stir emotion while my own soul was starving. I was part of the problem for longer than I’d like to admit. I didn’t lose my faith, but I lost my first love.

For almost a decade, I suffered from degenerative disc disease. The pain was relentless, sometimes dull, other times sharp and paralyzing. That constant distress wore on my soul as much as my body. Year after year, prayer after unanswered prayer, my faith waxed dim. I believed God loved me, but I just didn’t know if He _liked_ me. What I perceived as God’s distance eventually became my distance.

And that distance bred distraction.

In my early twenties, my life was full of good things. I was a husband and a father of two little girls. I worked as a software engineer. I served as a youth pastor and small-group leader. Ministry was demanding, but in those young years, my heart was still tender toward God. I loved seeing people encounter Jesus.

Somewhere along the way, though, pain and pressure wore me down. I stepped away from ministry entirely and threw myself into building a software business, while still working full-time. On paper, I was thriving. In reality, my soul was crumbling. I couldn’t yet see what my busyness was costing me. I attended church, but I brought my laptop and worked in the lobby. I numbed myself with sinful habits and worldly comforts. I told myself I was “hustling” and “providing,” but really, I was hiding. 

I told myself I was fine—until everything fell apart.

One evening, while lying on my couch, a sudden pain shot through my chest. As I started to hyperventilate, we rushed to the ER. I wondered if this was the end. It turned out to be a panic attack, not a heart attack. But the message was clear. My body had finally shouted what the Spirit had been whispering for years: _This is not the life I’ve called you to_.

At some point, in the quiet of my office, late at night, I dropped to my knees, undone by guilt. I cried out to God, repenting for chasing everything but Him. And God answered: _Lay down the business. Let go of the chase for glory._ And then, curiously, He added: _Stop working on Saturdays. Give that day to Me._ Only later would I understand what that last part actually meant.

After that scare, things began to shift, spiritually at least. My back was still a mess, but what disturbed me most wasn’t the physical pain; it was the life I wasted. I recall a time shortly after the ER, when I took my oldest daughter to a coffee shop, no agenda, just time. I bought her a cake pop. She sat on my lap, and we people-watched. It was a precious moment, but I couldn’t help but feel the weight of all my mistakes. 

I looked at her sweet five-year-old face and whispered, “I’m sorry I’ve missed so much.” She paused, thoughtfully. Then she smiled and chirped, “It’s okay, Daddy,” as she kissed me on the cheek and returned to her cake pop.

I won’t pretend I didn’t cry. 

I won’t pretend that remembering that now doesn’t make me cry again.

Dear friend, hear the lament of a broken man: I don’t remember my life from that season. I have photos. I have videos. But I have no memories. That haunts me. I keep the hospital bracelet from that night in the ER as a reminder. Not just of where I was, but of what I nearly lost. Forever.

Looking back now, I question if I even had saving faith. My stomach still churns thinking about that. That’s also why understanding the Mark of God is critical. 

For centuries, the Mark of the Beast has fueled speculation. This Mark is seen as an allegiance to Satan, identifying its bearer as a rebel against the Creator. But there’s a counterpart, equally significant, yet rarely taught: the Mark of God.

The Mark of God isn’t just some abstract symbol buried in ancient texts. It’s the evidence of genuine, living faith. It sets boundaries. It reminds us that faith comes first, but obedience **must** follow.

This reminds me of what the Apostle James warned us about:

> “But be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves.” (James 1:22)

### The Key to Understanding the Mark

A couple of years passed. My back continued to deteriorate, and every day felt heavier and more uncertain. By the end of it, going on disability seemed like the only option left. But in my worst hour, God intervened. 

I won’t tell that story here—not yet. I share it in full at the end of this book, because it deserves space. What matters for now is this: God healed what medicine could not. And when He did, He didn’t just restore my body, He rekindled my hunger for Him.

On the heels of my healing, God also stirred a deep burden to understand Revelation. In particular, I wanted to understand the Mark of the Beast. For me, this mystery became urgent, not academic, but personal. I spent hours a day reading and studying. I had to know why He sent me down this path. But as several months went by with no epiphany, I wondered if I misheard. Then I had a moment I’ll never forget.

I was rereading Revelation for the hundredth time, but as I got to 13:18, I heard the Spirit say, _pause_, after I read:

>“Here is **wisdom**. Let him who has **understanding** calculate the number of the beast...” (emphasis added)

And in that moment, I sensed the Spirit question me: _Do you have wisdom? Do you have understanding?_

I sighed aloud, _No_. And then it hit me: when you reach the end of a book and realize you don’t understand it, what do you do?

You start over.

So I did. I went back to Genesis and began rereading everything, even the genealogies. I continued reading, page after page, until I reached Exodus 13. And there, something clicked—the same language, _a mark on the hand and between the eyes_. As it turns out, the key to understanding Revelation 13:16 is found in Exodus 13:16.

I later learned this is what some call *expositional constancy*.[^1] This means that symbols in Scripture tend to carry consistent meaning. For example, the “beasts” in Daniel 7 represent nations, not monsters. This is made clear to Daniel by an angel. So when similar “beasts” appear in Revelation 13, the meaning remains the same. 

That may sound simple. But I was never taught to think this way. I grew up hearing endless theories about what the Mark of the Beast might be: social security numbers, RFID chips, tattoos, cryptocurrency. The more elaborate the explanation, the less it resonated.

I remember when our church brought in a guest teacher to do a deep dive on Revelation. He arrived with a sixty-foot mural packed with timelines, charts, and vivid scenes of the End Times. For a month, we walked through every bowl, trumpet, and seal. But when we got to the Mark of the Beast, his confidence wavered. He ended that night with a massive question mark. No firm answers. Just speculation.

He quoted Daniel a few times, but made little attempt to connect the imagery of Revelation back to the Old Testament. There was no mention of Exodus, no connection to the symbolism of God’s Mark. The story felt disjointed, like trying to finish a puzzle without the edge pieces.

That gap reflects something we’ve forgotten: We cannot understand what the Enemy has imitated unless we first understand what God has originated.

### The Pillars of the Mark

To help others understand the Mark of God, I like to use the Two Pillars analogy. The Mark of God stands on two unshakable pillars: a real relationship with God and a life of obedience to His commands. 

Jesus did not define salvation as an escape from hell or being a good person. He said eternal life is fellowship with the Father.[^2] But He also tied relationship to obedience:

>“If you love Me, keep My commandments” (John 14:15)

Those are the Two Pillars of the Mark of God, love and obedience. The Apostle John emphasized the Two Pillars similarly:

>“Now by this we know that we know Him, if we keep His commandments. He who says, ‘I know Him,’ and does not keep His commandments, is a liar, and the truth is not in him.” (1 John 2:3-4)

The truth of salvation is found in the tension between these Two Pillars. But knowing God must come first. From this knowing, obedience supernaturally flows. The first pillar is the wellspring of the second, and the second is the evidence of the first. Together, they form the Mark of God.

These twin truths will echo through every chapter ahead. From Exodus to Revelation, through Sabbath to Shema, every sign, seal, and Scripture points back to these pillars. They are the lens and the litmus. 

Not everyone will bear His Mark, not even those who call themselves Christian. Knowing God and knowing His Mark guards our hearts from distraction, deception, and drifting into a life we never meant to choose. It protects us from the subtle idolatry of success and from the slow-drip poison of compromise.

_Know Me. Know My Mark_, God whispers in what might be our final hour. Because if the Mark of God is legitimate, so is the counterfeit. But you can’t spot a counterfeit unless you know the real thing. And you won’t recognize the Beast unless you’ve truly walked with the Lamb.

[^1]: Expositional constancy is a term used primarily in evangelical circles to describe how symbols often carry consistent meanings when contextually appropriate.

[^2]: “And this is eternal life, that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent.” (John 17:3) 


## Chapter 2: The Mark of God - Born in Blood

>God’s seal upon my heart and hand,  
>A sign I walk as He commands,
>A Mark divine, on this I stand,
>To do all that such faith demands.

---

>“For indeed Christ, our Passover, was sacrificed for us.” (1 Corinthians 5:7)

---

Before the cross was lifted, before the tomb was empty, there was a door, sealed in sacrifice. On the night of Israel’s exodus, judgment swept through the land. Salvation didn’t come through strength or strategy. It came through a door. 

Jesus said, _“I am the door. If anyone enters by Me, he will be saved”_ (John 10:9). He wasn’t only speaking metaphorically; He was pointing back to Exodus, to lambs, to that night when God marked His people with a sign born in blood.

Throughout Scripture, the symbolism of the Mark of God is elegantly foreshadowed and richly repeated. Each mention adds to the beautiful and eternal significance of the Mark.

### The First Mention of the Mark of God

Exodus 13 is the very first mention of the Mark of God. And here, the broader message is that the Mark is a declaration that our only hope is in the Lamb. That our life is preserved not by what we’ve done, but by where we stand and who we stand under.

Exodus describes this Mark with vivid, layered language. The words carry not only the idea of a sign, but of remembrance, relationship, and rhythm.[^1]

>“It shall be as a sign to you on your hand and as a memorial between your eyes, that the Lord’s law may be in your mouth...” (Exodus 13:9)

>“It shall be as a sign on your hand and as frontlets between your eyes, for by strength of hand the Lord brought us out of Egypt.” (Exodus 13:16)

These references to God’s Mark in Exodus 13 connect it directly to the Passover. That chapter also declares that the feast of Passover should be an everlasting memorial for God’s people. Observing holy days like Passover served a dual purpose: to keep God’s Law near in heart and to remind His people of His incredible power to save. That power was never more intense than on the night of the first Passover.

### The First Passover

Long ago, the Israelites were a beacon of hope in Egypt. Through Joseph’s God-given wisdom, they had guided the nation of Egypt to unparalleled wealth during a worldwide famine, their favor shining like the sun over the Nile. 

But favor is fleeting. 

One pharaoh after Joseph’s generation, the favored had become the persecuted. And for over four hundred years, Israel would groan under the weight of increasingly oppressive Pharaohs, their bodies broken by slavery, their spirits crushed beneath the lash.

Then one day, Moses, a former prince of Egypt turned fugitive, returned with a brazen demand. At first, Moses pleaded for a meager day of rest; a plea to worship in the wilderness. But Pharaoh denied the request. And his refusal ignited the heavens. What began as a simple request became a permanent and powerful exodus. 

God responded with nine plagues, each a thunderous judgment on Egypt’s gods and a testament to His might. Rivers bled red, darkness devoured the daylight, and locusts stripped the land bare. With every plague, Egypt crumbled while Israel was preserved, a people set apart. Yet Pharaoh’s heart remained unyielding, a stone wall against a tide of divine will.

Then came the tenth plague, the most devastating and deadly. As the sun dipped below the horizon, an eerie hush settled over the land of Goshen. The Israelites, fearful yet obedient, hurried to follow Yahweh’s command. Each household sought a spotless one-year-old lamb. With careful hands, they sacrificed it. In steadfast obedience, they dipped hyssop in the lamb’s lifeblood and stained their doorposts, an emblem of trust in God’s promise. 

They roasted the meat, but ate it in haste, shoes on their feet, staffs in hand, packed and ready to flee. The bitter herbs stung their tongues as a reminder of their suffering, while unleavened bread spoke of the urgency of their escape. They were ready to flee, yet they were commanded to remain. No one dared step beyond their crimson doors—for the angel of death was near.

Outside, the night grew heavy with dread as wails in Egypt pierced the silence. All the firstborn fell, from the palace to the poorest home. But in Goshen, where the blood stood guard, death _passed over_.

This blood‑sign echoes the language of the Mark of God.[^2] It served as both a physical and symbolic sign of their alignment with Yahweh.

> “Now the blood shall be a sign for you on the houses where you are. And when I see the blood, I will pass over you; and the plague shall not be on you to destroy you when I strike the land of Egypt.” (Exodus 12:13)

The sacrifice of Christ, like the Passover, gives us victory over death.[^3] It sets the believer apart, marking them as His beloved. A seal and a declaration: _These are My people_.

But the blood was more than a seal; it was an act of faith. 

Can you imagine that test? A mother’s trembling hand as she painted blood on a door frame, staking everything on a lamb.

For us today, that tremble is felt too: in quiet Communion, in a meal shared with sacred gratitude. We renew our surrender with tears, trusting Him to pass over our failures.

This is the Mark of God, born in blood, remembered in surrender.

Just as the blood is a sign of our salvation, the Sabbath is a sign of our belonging. And if the Passover declares our deliverance, the Shema declares our devotion. Next, we’ll see how the Sabbath and the Shema illustrate the fullness of knowing God and bearing His Mark with distinction.

[^1]: Biblical authors used several terms to describe the Mark of God. The most common term is translated as _“sign”_ (Hebrew _‘ôt_), meaning a signal, a distinguishing mark, or evidence. The second most common is _“memorial,”_ meaning a reminder, record, or remembrance. Lastly, there is the word _“frontlets,”_ which can refer to a band, a mark, or, later, a phylactery—known today as _tefillin_ (Deuteronomy 6:8 and 11:18).

[^2]: The word “sign” in this verse is the same word that describes God’s Mark in several other verses. 

[^3]: In the New Testament, this theme finds its ultimate fulfillment in Jesus, the Lamb of God. John 1:29 and especially 1 Corinthians 5:7: “For indeed Christ, our Passover, was sacrificed for us.” Later in Revelation 12:11, we are reminded of the triumph found through His sacrifice: “And they overcame him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony, and they did not love their lives to the death.” 

## Chapter 3: The Mark of God - Sabbath and Shema

>In the hush of the seventh day,
>A stillness breaks the world’s loud sway.
>A sign, a seal, a sacred rest,
>Where heaven meets the heart’s request.

---

>“...Surely My Sabbaths you shall keep, for it is a sign between Me and you throughout your generations, that you may know that I am the Lord who sanctifies you.” (Exodus 31:13)

---

Even before I called myself a Christian, I sensed something reverent about the weekend. Work gives way to rest, and something in the air changes. Most of us have felt that quiet anticipation that builds in as Friday night approaches. The world exhales. Students drop their books. Workers rush home. We long to breathe again. It’s a rhythm deeper than we realize, etched into creation itself.

Ironically, it was only as a Christian that the Sabbath lost its meaning for me. In my lust for more, I wore busyness like a badge. Ceasing felt like sinning. And because of that, I didn’t hear His voice; I drowned it out. I missed what mattered most: my soul’s quiet, my family’s laughter, my God’s closeness.

Back then, church was a service, not a gathering. It meant performance over presence. I agonized over every detail, whether my transitions were smooth, my prayers polished, my message tight. The team felt it too. Sunday became a flurry of tasks, a checklist dressed as worship. But that wasn’t rest. It was a hamster wheel inside a locked cage.

But the Sabbath set me free.

Now, when that sacred day arrives, it feels like an invitation. Most Friday nights settle with a quiet meal with the family. The next morning, our little church gathers, not just out of habit, but out of hunger. We begin in a circle and recite the Shema, our pledge of allegiance to God. It’s an ancient prayer that’s been whispered across the ages. Little hands join in with motions to the words. It’s a modest ritual, but it’s how we prepare our hearts for the holy.

What follows is more food, more fellowship, but less rush, more hush. Conversations linger long, worship is sweet, and prayer is filled with pause. The Holy Spirit is the real teacher. His voice is the only one we long for. This is Sabbath delight. It’s both a Law and a lifeline.

Is it any wonder God calls it a “sign”?

### The Mark of God in the Sabbath

> “It is a sign between Me and the children of Israel forever; for in six days the LORD made the heavens and the earth, and on the seventh day He rested and was refreshed.” (Exodus 31:17)

Even God paused. Even He was “refreshed” by the Sabbath. What a mystery.[^1]

The Sabbath isn’t a fleeting festival. It’s primal, born when God rested on the seventh day, and carved into the Ten Commandments. That day, whether in silence or song, marks us.[^2] And when we enter it with faith and fellowship, we witness the beauty of the Two Pillars—loving God and walking in His ways. We rest as He rested. We are refreshed as He was refreshed.

The Sabbath also isn’t a burden, as some suppose. The prophet Isaiah pleads with us to call it a “delight”.[^3] This is especially true after Israel emerged from generations of slavery. The Sabbath became a privilege, but it was also an act of defiance against the tyranny of endless toil. Today, it’s no less radical. In a society that glorifies grind, Sabbath is rebellion. It says: _I am not defined by production. Pharaoh does not own me. I belong to Yahweh_.[^4]

If the Sabbath is a resistance against a culture that clamors for more, the Shema is a signet of our devotion.

### The Mark of God in the Shema

The Shema is a pledge encapsulating the Two Pillars of the Mark. This prayer weaves together love and loyalty, mind and motive:

> “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one! You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength...You shall bind them as a sign on your hand and they shall be as frontlets between your eyes.” (Deuteronomy 6:4–8)

The Shema isn’t just words; it’s a war cry. It thunders. It commands love with every heartbeat, every breath, every choice. In a world of lesser gods pining for our loyalty, the Shema plants a flag: _I am His._ 

> “Therefore you shall lay up these words of Mine in your heart and in your soul, and bind them as a sign on your hand, and they shall be as frontlets between your eyes.” (Deuteronomy 11:18)

This theme of divine marking threads throughout the Scriptures like golden embroidery.[^5] These signs, Sabbath and Shema, are part of God’s signature on His people. Combined with the Passover, they express His design for covenant and communion. And as we’ll see in the visions of Ezekiel, God takes His Mark seriously.

And so should we.

## Chapter 4: The Mark of God - Engraved in Grief

> Who weeps for sin with heartfelt tears?
> Who guards His Word in holy fear?
> He whose soul is crushed and broken,
> He who heeds all God has spoken.

---

> “For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.” (2 Corinthians 7:10)

---

In Israel’s darkest hour, Ezekiel kept watch. The covenant was abandoned. The sanctuary was defiled. Hearts once warm with worship had grown as cold as the stone idols they now served. Because of all this, judgment loomed like a gathering storm.

Amid that spiritual decay, Ezekiel received a vision with a clear message: sin is serious, and so is the Mark of God. His vision is both haunting and hopeful. It reveals that bearing God’s Mark means more than merely claiming to know Him; it means sharing His grief over sin.

The vision in Ezekiel 9 describes a man clothed in linen whom God commands to mark the foreheads of all who “sigh and cry” over the sins of Israel. After this, destroyers armed with judgment follow; they are commanded to slay all who lack the mark. And hear this, church leaders: God said to begin with the elders in His sanctuary.

What set those marked ones apart? Not flawless lives, but broken hearts. 

### Godly Sorrow vs Guilt

We often mistake guilt for godly sorrow, but God doesn’t desire mere worldly regret—He calls us to repentance. As 2 Corinthians 7:10 declares, only godly sorrow leads to salvation. Godly sorrow isn’t about the shame of being exposed, but the grief of having wounded a loving God. It means feeling the weight of Christ’s sacrifice—the pain of what He endured to redeem us.

Yet we cannot grasp the gravity of His grace until we’ve acknowledged the depravity of our sin. That is what godly sorrow is: allowing that uncomfortable anguish to cut through us like a surgeon’s scalpel—precise, painful, but meant to heal. It is not natural to embrace such vulnerable honesty, but that is where genuine change begins.

Guilt and shame are what are natural, even easy. I’ve felt guilt a thousand times. Shame? It’s been like my shadow. But godly sorrow? I’ve experienced that exactly twice in my whole life. Not with tears alone, but with terror. The kind that says: _I cannot—must not—remain the same_.

When we’re comfortable in sin, it’s only because we’re distracted. Nobody stares into the depths of their iniquity and shrugs. We run. We hide. We busy ourselves with anything to keep from feeling. And when we dodge the Spirit’s conviction, we will falter in leading others to repentance as well. 

### A Leadership Low

I’ll never forget one night in a small group. I was the leader, yet I felt like a fraud. Another man shared his struggle, then looked right at me and asked, “How can I be free?” 

I froze.

I didn’t have an honest answer. Because I was still wrestling with the same sin. Instead of truth, I offered platitudes, something about grace, “we all struggle.” And while that might sound like compassion, it wasn’t. I didn’t want him to feel convicted, because _I_ didn’t want to feel convicted.

I hate thinking back to that leadership low. It’s disturbing not just because my answer was weak, but because I was so dishonest with him and with myself. The truth is, godly sorrow demands honesty. And if we sigh and cry, if we weep and repent, we can feel safe letting our healed wounds point to Him. Our testimony might be exactly what someone else needs to hear to believe that God can heal them, too. It’s sobering to realize that someone else’s freedom might be waiting on ours.

Ezekiel’s vision weighs heavily, as it must. It foreshadows a day of reckoning, possibly in our own time. It points forward to a people who bear His name—and today, that means us. Let us look inward, but let us also look back: to those who walked before us, to those who walked with Him, to those whose lives still echo through eternity, their faith a lasting testament to us all.


## Chapter 5: The Mark of God - Echoed in Eternity

>In lives of saints, His Mark is shown,  
A holy bond, forever known. 
Their faithful love, a steadfast flame,
Marked by God, their lives proclaim.

---

>“…For he who comes to God must believe that He is, and that He is a rewarder of those who diligently seek Him.” (Hebrews 11:6)

---

Scripture is filled with people who bore the Mark of God, not a visible sign, but a sacred bond revealed through faith. From a wandering patriarch to a poet-king to a pagan prostitute, figures like Abraham, David, and Rahab each uniquely carried this Mark. What did it mean for them, and what might it mean for us?

The Mark of God is nestled in the very heart of the Ten Commandments. In Exodus 20:5-6, God declares:

> “...For I, the Lord your God, am a jealous God... but showing mercy to thousands, to those who love Me and keep My commandments.”

This is more than a warning against idolatry; it’s an invitation into a covenant. God promises enduring mercy to those who love Him and walk in His ways—a blessing not just for one life, but for generations.

Throughout Scripture, we see this promise unfold in flesh and blood. These stories remind us that bearing God’s Mark isn’t about theory but trust and a heart willing to follow Him, even when it costs everything. Each story unfolds against stone: an altar, a cave, an idol. These are not just settings; they are sacred thresholds where faith is tested, and the Mark of God is revealed.

Let’s begin with Abraham, whose trust in God was tried in a way that would shake any heart, and yet his obedience remained unshaken.

### Abraham: A Father’s Radical Faith

God woke Abraham with an unexpected and unwelcome command: “Take your only son, Isaac, whom you love, and offer him as a burnt offering” [^7.5]. Abraham lay frozen, eyes locked on the tent’s dark ceiling, his breath shallow. 

Was this the same God who promised Isaac?

Pagan gods devoured children on fiery altars; could Yahweh ask the same?

He clutched the blanket, sweat beading on his brow, wishing the voice would fade like a dream. But it lingered, heavy as a boulder. 

As Abraham reluctantly accepted this command, he gently woke Isaac and saddled up the donkey. They set out on a three-day journey to Mount Moriah—three long days to make up any excuse to turn back.

Isaac walked beside him, the knife for the sacrifice slung at his side, its gentle clinging a quiet menace. _Does he know?_ Abraham’s mind churned. _Has he noticed there is no lamb for offering?_

On the third day, the hill of sacrifice rose into view, a hill not unlike Golgotha. Father and son ascended alone. Isaac bore the wood on his back, like Christ carrying His cross. At the summit, the world fell flat. Abraham stumbled to build the altar with angry hands, slowly stacking stones as if he could delay the inevitable. Isaac watched curiously. “Father,” he said, in his small voice, “where is the lamb?”

The words struck like a fist. His gaze locked with Isaac’s. “God will provide,” he whispered. _God will provide._ He had to believe it, even as his heart screamed otherwise.

They finished the altar together, and Abraham’s voice broke the awkward silence. “Lie down.”

Isaac hesitated, a flicker of confusion on his face, but he climbed onto the wood, trusting as ever. Abraham bound his wrists; each knot in the rope bound a knot in Abraham’s chest. Isaac lay still, his eyes never leaving his father’s. 

Abraham felt Isaac’s stare but dared not acknowledge his trusting gaze.

Then, he lifted the knife, his arm trembling as the blade caught the sun in a cruel flash. “Oh God, Oh God, Oh God,” Abraham chanted frantically. _Surely God didn’t mean this. Where is He?_ Abraham thought. 

Then the wind stopped. Time hung, taut as a bow. Abraham’s throat was hoarse and dry. He couldn’t even swallow. 

There wasn’t a catch. 

This was it. 

Obey or not obey. 

Abraham closed his eyes and focused his heart on the God of the impossible. “Grant me strength,” he whispered, preparing to let that knife do the unthinkable. 

Abraham gripped the knife with both hands, then, a shout: “Abraham!”

He froze. 

The knife hovered a moment before gingerly falling out of his hand.

>“Do not lay your hand on the lad...for now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your son, your only _son,_ from Me.” (Genesis 22:12)

Abraham melted to his knees and sobbed with increasingly great fervor. 

Isaac sat up, unbound, his hands reaching for his father. In the thicket, a ram thrashed, its horns tangled, its bleats a hymn of mercy.

This is the test of God’s Mark, a trust forged in the fire of obedience.

Abraham bore the Mark of God through radical faith so profound it shaped the destiny of nations. Not passive belief, but faith willing to lift the knife. 

James reminds us that saving faith doesn’t just believe God exists; it follows with worthy action:

>“You believe that there is one God... Even the demons believe—and tremble!” (James 2:19)

Genuine faith always bears fruit, and obedience is that fruit.

>“By faith Abraham obeyed...” (Hebrews 11:8)

Here again, we see the Two Pillars: Abraham trusted the voice of the God—his friend —and obeyed. 

In Genesis 26, God reaffirmed his covenant to Isaac and reminded Isaac why this blessed promise was passed down. It wasn’t just because of Abraham’s faith, listen to why:

>“…because Abraham obeyed My voice and kept My charge, My commandments, My statutes, and My laws.” (Genesis 26:5)

From Abraham’s faith came a lineage of promise, including David, a shepherd boy whose heart, though flawed, chased after God’s own.

### David: Shepherd, Saint, and Psalmist

David settled into a cave, dark, damp, forgotten by men but not by God. And not by the king who hunted him.

King David was Israel’s rightful anointed, yet he lived like a fugitive. Years of exile had stripped him of comfort and certainty. David had been chased through deserts, betrayed by friends, and nearly killed on more than one occasion. And still, he waited, not out of fear, but out of faith.

Cold rock leached the heat from his body; sleep came between shivers. The air smelled of corroded limestone, with the sour tang of sweat. Water ticked from the ceiling like a slow clock, but just enough off rhythm to be more annoying than comforting. The dark was depressing, and the attitude of David and his men grew tense. They despised Saul, the once‑anointed king, who had driven them into this hole in the ground.

But then came a day of reckoning.

Saul approached David’s cave unaware as his shadow stretched across the jagged stone floor. He entered alone, seeking privacy, unaware that David and his mighty men lay just beyond the reach of the light—watching.

This was the chance to end the chase, the crown, at last, within reach.

David’s men whispered urgently: _“Now is the time. Strike him down.”_ And why not? Saul had hurled spears at David twice. He had pursued him unjustly, tormented by jealousy and madness. Killing Saul now wouldn’t just be self-defense; it would be justice. Nobody else would think twice.

David moved silently toward Saul, blade drawn. But he paused, not for fear of Saul, but for fear of God. He lowered the dagger and cut only the corner of Saul’s robe.

As Saul departed, David called after him, holding the torn cloth as evidence of mercy. His words echoed through the ravine like a psalm shouted aloud: _“I will not stretch out my hand against the Lord’s anointed.”_ [^7.4]

David was resolved: God alone appoints kings, and God alone removes them.

This is the test of the Mark of God, not victory over enemies, but victory over self. David passed that test in the cave. Where Saul followed ambition, David followed the Lord. He was not perfect, far from it. But he never stopped pursuing the True Shepherd.

Forged in lonely pastures and refined in quiet caves, David became a man after God’s own heart (Acts 13:22). David bore the Mark of God through passionate pursuit. In love, he obeyed; this is once again, the Two Pillars.

His psalms poured out like fire from a volcano: raw, unfiltered, and soul-deep. 

> “O God, You are my God;  
> Early will I seek You;  
> My soul thirsts for You;  
> My flesh longs for You  
> In a dry and thirsty land  
> Where there is no water…  
> Because Your lovingkindness is better than life,  
> My lips shall praise You.” 
> (Psalm 63:1–3)

“Lovingkindness,” the same word used to describe the promise of those marked by God in Exodus 20:6. Like Abraham, David’s obedience carried generational weight. His worship shaped a kingdom. God remembered his passion. Again and again, long after David’s reign, Scripture speaks of mercy shown to future generations _for David’s sake_ [^7.1].

> “…for the sake of My servant David, whom I chose because he kept My commandments and My statutes.” (1 Kings 11:34)

This is the Mark: a longing that refuses to die, even in lonely caves, even in the absence of justice. Perhaps this relentless pursuit ran deeper than David knew—an echo from his great-great-grandmother Rahab, the former pagan prostitute who chose the One True God.

### Rahab: Courageous Trust

Rahab bore the Mark of God through courageous faith. She risked everything for a God she had only heard of. God brought Israel out of Egypt with a power that shook the world. The greatest army on earth vanished beneath the Red Sea. Ten terrifying plagues judged Egypt and its gods. In the fortified walls of Jericho, a prostitute named Rahab confessed that Jericho’s heart melted at the news [^7.6].

There was certified panic in Jericho. The heat of looming war hung like a curse. From her high window, Rahab watched soldiers sweep the streets, eyes sharp, hands twitching. Word had spread: Israelite spies were inside the city. Their rumored presence felt like a lethal infestation.

The king’s command was swift. Find them. Kill them. Kill anyone who aids them, too.

Rahab’s home, once a place of seductive secrets, now harbored a deadly kind. She had taken in the strangers. Their accents gave them away. She knew who they were. And she had heard the stories of their God.

The God of Israel was no stone idol. He moved. He _judged_.

She had lived her whole life surrounded by gods, bloodthirsty idols who demanded children on burning altars, who ruled through fear and lust. She had seen what happened to dissenters. A woman like her had no shelter; she was expendable.

And now the king’s men were coming. They would pound on every door. Search every room. If she were caught harboring spies, her death would be public and horrific.

She could have handed them over. Claimed ignorance. Maybe even been rewarded. That would have been expected. That would have been _safe_.

But something deeper stirred within her.

She wasn’t just defying a king but abandoning her gods, culture, and people. Standing in the fracture of a collapsing worldview, she placed everything on a whisper of hope: that the God of Israel, the God who sees, the God who saves—might show her mercy.

Rahab hid the spies beneath flax on her roof and waited for the soldiers to finish their frantic interrogation. Though she barely knew God, she trusted Him and risked everything to follow Him. The Two Pillars were expressed through love and loyalty.

A scarlet cord, a thin thread of faith, was hung from her window as a sign. As it fluttered in the wind, surely she held her breath. What if someone noticed? Her heart stopped a hundred times in the days between promise and deliverance.

Rahab’s story in Joshua 2 is striking. She wasn’t an Israelite, and she wasn’t raised with the Law or the Covenant. She lived in one of the most abominable pagan societies in history, and she was known for her sin. Yet something about Yahweh gripped her heart. Her faith, based solely on the reports of Yahweh’s deeds, mirrors the blessedness Jesus attributed to those who believe without seeing (John 20:29). 

After she hid the spies and officially risked all, she pleaded for a promise. Since she showed kindness, she asked that they would show kindness to her and her household (Joshua 2:12). The word “kindness” in Joshua 2:12 is the same “mercy” of God’s generational promise in Exodus 20:6 [^7.3]. Rahab innately understood Yahweh’s covenantal character, a desire for a relationship beyond ethnicity or culture, a promise open to anyone who would trust in His name. 

Rahab also asked for a “true token,” the same word used for God’s “mark” in Exodus 13:9 [^7.2]. The cord was that token. In a city marked for judgment, it echoed the blood on Israel’s doorposts during Passover. And in every age, God still calls His people out before the walls fall [^7.7]. It became a sign of salvation, a scarlet thread in the tapestry of redemption, foreshadowing the blood of Christ.

God marks those who fear Him, not those merely born into covenant, but those who _choose_ covenant.

Our Messiah came from the bloodline of these heroes. Their stories still speak. They remind us that God’s Mark transcends time, culture, and lineage. It is an emblem of the soul.

But their lives also confront us with a question: 
**How are we marked?**

Not everyone who professes faith will enter the kingdom. The prophets drew a clear line between those who _bear_ the Mark and those who _miss_ it. These stories aren’t just inspirational, they’re instructional. They counsel us toward intimacy with God and warn us against complacency and shallow faith.

**Faith**—that’s where the Mark of God begins: childlike in its simplicity, yet costly in its surrender.  
**Complacency**—that’s where the Mark of the Beast begins: rooted in rebellion, content to serve self.

Oh, that the things written about Abraham, David, and Rahab would one day be written about us:

Radical faith that obeys.
Persistent passion.
Courage to choose the living God.

Together, their lives form a portrait of what it means to be marked by God. Their faith and obedience will echo in eternity. But what does obedience look like for us? In some ways, it looks the same as it did for Abraham who obeyed the Law of God (Genesis 26:5).  

But what **is** the Law of God? And what does it mean to obey Him today?

To answer that, we must untangle a confusion that has crept into the Church—the difference between the **Law of God** and the law of Moses. These two laws are often conflated, yet profoundly different in purpose, permanence, and power.

To know the Law of God is to know the heart of God—because His commands are rooted in His love. And in a world of lawlessness, that clarity means everything.

The shape of God’s divinity.

>“There is a real Law, which none of us made, but which we find pressing on us.” - C.S. Lewis [^10.1]

In the beginning, God formed man from the dust of the earth and breathed into his nostrils the breath of Life. That breath was more than oxygen—it was glory, likeness, and righteousness.

Adam rose not as a beast of instinct but as an image-bearer of the eternal, perfectly righteous Creator. The beasts were made to roam. The stars were made to shine. But Adam was made to rule.

God not only gave Adam authority to rule, but He also imprinted His moral nature upon him. His Law was not an external code but an internal compass, etched into Adam’s very being. He intuitively knew that love was greater than hate, murder was rebellion, and that to sin was ruin. Before stone tablets, the Law of God was alive in Adam.

Man was the crescendo of creation, but the finale was divine rest—the Sabbath. God blessed the seventh day and rested, not because He was tired, but because creation was complete and communion with man had begun.

But sin shattered that communion.

Centuries later, the echo of that inner Law rang again—this time not in a garden, but on a trembling mountain. At Sinai, God did not create a new standard; He reaffirmed the eternal one:

The mountain quaked and moaned.

The sky cracked with lightning.

Thick black smoke billowed like a furnace. 

Holy fire danced upon the summit.

> “So it came about on the third day, when it was morning, that there were thunder and lightning flashes and a thick cloud upon the mountain… and the mountain quaked violently.” (Exodus 19:16–18)

Then God gave a command—so severe, so final—that it silenced even curiosity:

> “Do not go near the mountain. Do not let even a beast touch it, or it shall be stoned.”

And then, from that fiery mountain, **God spoke**. Not Moses. Not from man but from Majesty itself—the voice that had formed matter now thundered *Ten Words* (Exodus 20:1).

To the Hebrew people, they were not just commands. They were **utterances**—divine declarations from the heart of the Almighty.

And this is where we must begin: not with religion, but with a holy God revealing Himself in fire, love, and justice. The Ten Commandments are not rules. They are a revelation of the One who gave them.

### The Two Laws

The Bible often speaks of “the law,” but without careful attention, we can conflate two very different covenants: the **eternal Law of God** and the **temporary law of Moses**. Like threads in a tapestry, they can appear as one from a distance. But when we look closely, we find two distinct patterns—one eternal, the other fulfilled.

Understanding this distinction is not a matter of theological trivia; it’s essential to our faith. God’s Law was never meant to enslave. It was always meant to reveal His holiness, expose our sin, and point us to our need for a Savior. From Adam to Abraham, the eternal Law—rooted in love and righteousness—has never changed. [^10.2]

But with Moses, God gave Israel another set of laws: **ceremonial**, **civil**, and **dietary**. They were designed to govern Israel as a distinct nation and foreshadow Christ. These laws had a divine purpose—but a temporary one. Once fulfilled in Christ, they were no longer binding on Gentile believers.

### The Council of Jerusalem

The Council of Jerusalem marked more than a doctrinal debate—it defined the shape of the gospel for all generations. 

Picture the scene: a room in Jerusalem buzzing with tension. Hungry and tired because of much prayer and fasting, the apostles faced a critical challenge. The question was urgent, and divisive: **Must Gentile believers follow the law of Moses to be saved?**

This was the doctrine of those called Judaizers:

> “Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved.” (Acts 15:1)

This error placed rituals and externals at the center of salvation rather than the Cross. But the Apostles, guided by the Holy Spirit, rejected that burden. They proclaimed what remains essential—and what has been fulfilled.

Yet today, some misuse the Council’s decision to reject **all** law, including God’s moral Law. That was never the intent. And still today, others echo the same error as Judaizers.

Judaizers didn’t vanish in the first century. I met one years before I came to know Christ.

He wasn’t exactly a friend—more like a friend-of-a-friend. One hot summer afternoon, I was hiding on a rooftop smoking a cigarette. He craned his head through the window and said, “You know, smoking is a sin.”

I raised an eyebrow. “Really? Why?”

“Your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit,” he said. “And smoking destroys the temple.”

I paused, irritated. Then I took a slow, defiant puff and exhaled toward him. His words felt hollow—religious, and missing the point entirely.

Years later, after Christ had transformed my life, I ran into him again. 

I reminded him of that rooftop exchange. He laughed, uneasy. Then I told him what he missed: he spoke of sin, but not of salvation—rules without a Redeemer. That’s the danger of modern-day legalism. 

That’s not to say the law has no place; it does. But it’s critical to understand which law applies to all people for all time, and which was meant as a temporary tutor to point us to Christ.

### Two Laws: One Eternal, One Fulfilled

The **Law of God**—founded in the Ten Commandments and encapsulated in the Greatest Commandments—has always reflected God’s character.

The **law of Moses**, in contrast, was given for a specific time, to a specific people, and for a particular purpose: to set Israel apart, and most importantly, to point toward the Messiah.

Some teachers have dismissed the Ten Commandments as irrelevant for Christians. They claim that the Old Testament was not the go-to source regarding Church behavior. It’s argued that we are not accountable to the Ten Commandments because that is the Jewish law. Some have even blasphemously said: “Thou shalt not obey the Ten Commandments” [^10.3]

Such a message holds weight only if we deny the crucial distinction between the Two Laws. Without that distinction, we dissolve the substance of our faith—an error used to justify moral subjectivity.

To help clarify the difference, here are ten key contrasts between the law of Moses and the Law of God:

> The **Law of God** is like the compass—it points to true north.  
> The **law of Moses** is like the map for a specific leg of the journey—helpful, but no longer needed once you arrive at the destination.

We are not justified by obeying the Law—but neither is our obedience irrelevant. If we blur that line, we risk returning to legalism—or drifting into lawlessness.

God’s Law still matters—not to save, but to shape. It reveals what pleases Him. It did not die at the Cross. It was fulfilled—and lives still, not in stone, but in hearts made new.

In the next chapter, we’ll explore exactly how that happens—how God writes His Law on our hearts and empowers us to walk in it through the Spirit.

## Chapter 7: The Two Tablets


>God’s Law, the soul’s north star,
>Still guides both near and far.
>Engraved since creation,
>Love’s sacred narration.

> “On these two commandments hang all the Law and the Prophets.” (Matthew 22:40)

---

These two tablets, given to Moses, were not just stones. They were the bedrock of relational covenant. The first tablet carved out the way we love God, and the second, how we love one another. These aren’t just commands to obey; they are the Mark of those who follow a good King.

Yet, when we read the English version of the Ten Commandments, we often imagine Charlton Heston’s booming voice declaring “THOU SHALT NOT…” But in Hebrew, the tone is more like a mission statement—not just about inhibition but identity.

Just like I used to discipline my daughters, I wouldn’t just say “Stop that,” or “No, Ma’am,” but I’d gently whisper, “My Maci is kind. My Maci doesn’t bite.” 

I wanted my girls to see themselves differently. I wanted them to express the identity I knew was within them. That’s also how God speaks to His people:

> “My people do not murder.”
> “My people have no other gods before Me.”

> “He who has My commandments and keeps them, it is he who loves Me.” (John 14:21)

When Jesus was asked, “Which is the greatest commandment?” He didn’t quote one of the Ten. Instead, He pointed to the Shema—the ancient Jewish confession of faith:

> “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one! You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength.”  (Deuteronomy 6:4-5)

Jesus said this is the Greatest Commandment, not because it replaces the Ten, but because it defines them. Every commandment God gave flows from this love. The first four show us how to love God; the last six show us how to love our neighbor. The entire Law hinges on these two relationships.

However, the proof of passion is priority.

### Love the Lord Your God

### Other Gods

**Priority** means the state of being first. [^11.1]

We always make time for what we love. If we love God, He won’t get the leftovers of our day. He will be our first thought in the morning and our last at night.

“Priority” comes from “prior”—what comes before. And God declares in the first commandment:

> “You shall have no other gods **before** Me.” (Exodus 20:3 emphasis added)

This isn’t about God being first in a ranking system. It’s about removing all rivals.

Imagine if my wife asked, “Have no other women before me,” and I took that to mean she gets the **most attention**, but not the **only affection**. Imagine if I thought it acceptable to have pictures of other women adorning my home or my phone as long as the images of her were most prominent. 

Would such division be counted as devotion?

That’s absurd. And yet, that’s how many treat God.

Jesus made it clear:

> “No one can serve two masters.” (Matthew 6:24)

For me, this means beginning every day in His presence. It wasn’t easy building this practice. It took nearly a year before my time with Him felt consistent and meaningful. But now, I can’t imagine life without Him being first. That time has become my primary source of insight and intimacy. 

Time. The best of our time. That is how love is proven—by what comes first.

What do you do **before** you do anything else?

When He’s not first and only, the door of our heart remains open for others to intrude. 

What happens when our affections drift? When love turns to something or someone lesser?

That is the warning of the second command.

### Idols

>“You shall not make for yourself a carved image—any likeness of anything that is in heaven above, or that is in the earth...” (Exodus 20:4)

Idolatry can be subtle. It doesn’t always take the form of a golden calf. Idols tend to surface when our passion for the eternal cools. They awaken when we drift to sleep. We may dress them up to appear as something good, but whatever captures our **attention** and **affection**, is just an idol.

J.R.R. Tolkien painted this powerfully in The Lord of the Rings. The tormented Golem, a once humble hobbit, clung to the One Ring for generations until it consumed him entirely. He didn’t just possess it—it possessed him. So much so that he eerily called it his “Precious.”

Idols are always precious to us. But they do not love us back. They dominate, distort, and demand. We become all-consumed, even codependent, with the shiny ruler of our heart.

I see this play out every time I give my dog, Jake, a new bone. He clutches it with his tiny paws, hoards it in a corner, and growls if I come too close. In that moment, the bone is everything. He forgets my voice. All he sees is the bone.

He still knows I’m his master, but in that moment—the bone rules him.

Isn’t that exactly how idolatry works?  
We say we belong to God.  
But our hearts are wrapped around something else.

And like Jake with his bone, we can become so lost in lesser things that we forget the voice of our Master.

But here’s the good news:  
God doesn’t just warn us about idols—He offers us something better.  
He calls us into a love so visceral and complete that no counterfeit could ever compare.

He is proud to be our God. He desires us to bear His name, just as a father or husband gives his name in covenant. But when we live in contradiction to His name, we blaspheme our God, we defy our Master.

That’s what it means to break the third commandment.

### His Name

> “You shall not take the name of the Lord your God in vain, for the Lord will not hold him guiltless who takes His name in vain.” (Exodus 20:7)

When most people think of the Third Commandment, they imagine it’s about not using “God” as a curse word. While careless speech is undoubtedly an issue, reducing this commandment to “Don’t say OMG” misses the weight of what God is commanding.

The actual offense is not in _speaking_, but in **taking** His name in vain.

The Hebrew word translated **“take”** (_nasa’_) means to bear, carry, or lift.  
The word for **“vain”** (_shav’_) means emptiness, falsehood, or worthlessness.  
To take God’s name in vain is to bear it falsely, as if it has no weight, power, or truth.

The word “rabbi” means **my master**. To follow a rabbi is an act of complete surrender. His teachings become your worldview. His life becomes your way of life. His name becomes your banner.

That’s what it means to take the name of God. Anything less makes His name worthless.

This is why Jesus asked,

> “Why do you call Me ‘Lord, Lord,’ and not do the things which I say?”  (Luke 6:46)

And the Third Commandment ends with a just warning:

> “For the Lord will not hold him guiltless who takes His name in vain.”

No rabbi would tolerate a disciple who bore his name and defiled his teaching. And God does not excuse those who misrepresent Him either.

Tragically, some of the deepest wounds to the name of God have not come from the mouths of atheists, but from the lives of His people. This is especially true of leaders. False shepherds who distort the truth and commit grievous sin have damaged God’s name more than any demon in Hell ever could.

When this happens, the world doesn’t just see a fallen man—they see a failed God.

Paul’s rebuke to Israel still echoes today:

> “The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you.” (Romans 2:24)

This is the great betrayal.  
Not merely that the world mocks it’s Creator,  
but that His people have given them a reason to.

That’s a heavy thought, I know. 

But imagine if every person who bore His name actually lived like Jesus?

What if Christians weren’t known for hypocrisy, but for radical love, integrity, and holiness?

What if we made His name beautiful again?

It begs the question:  
Would someone glorify God by examining your life?

This is the light we’re called to be—torches of supernatural grace and power, burning so brightly that others only see the goodness of Abba in us.

>“Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works and glorify your Father in heaven.” (Matthew 5:16)

The Third Commandment is about more than words—it is about **witness**.  

To carry His name rightly is to live by His rhythm. And part of that rhythm is rest. When we honor the Sabbath, we honor His rule—we cease and remember who rules our heart.

### The Sabbath

>“Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy. Six days you shall labor and do all your work, but the seventh day is the Sabbath of the Lord your God. In it you shall do no work...” (Exodus 20:8-10)

What is the opposite of rest? Restlessness. 

We live in a restless world. We pride ourselves on our busyness, wear exhaustion like a badge of honor, and call it success. But what if the pace of our lives is evidence of something more profound? A spiritual disconnection.

What if we’ve forgotten one of God’s greatest gifts—the Sabbath?

For most of my life, I treated the Sabbath as irrelevant or optional. I assumed it was a relic of the Old Testament, which didn’t apply to me because I lived under grace. After all, didn’t Jesus fulfill the law? Didn’t Paul say every day is the same? I clung to those interpretations, though something didn’t sit right deep down. I had embraced a version of Christianity that celebrated freedom while quietly ignoring obedience.

It wasn’t until God brought me to my knees that I began to see the Sabbath differently. He told me to stop working on Saturdays and set that time apart for Him. At first, it felt strange—unnecessary, even. But I obeyed. And in doing so, I began to experience the healing and restoration that only God can offer. I am convinced the same will be true for you as well.

The Sabbath is an invitation to rest in His love and to trust Him to be enough. To let go of our endless striving. What some see as an outdated commandment, I invite you to see as a promise, a sacred rhythm that refreshes the soul.

> “Blessed is the man who does this, and the son of man who lays hold on it; who keeps from defiling the Sabbath, and keeps his hand from doing any evil.” (Isaiah 56:2)

When we honor the Sabbath, we honor God. We declare He is our Creator, Provider, and Rest. But Sabbath is more than one restful day, it should reshape our entire week. Rest doesn’t just begin on the seventh day; it spills backward into the six. To fully receive the gift of Sabbath, we must begin to slow the pace of everything else. 

Rabbi Abraham Heschel famously described the Sabbath as a “palace in time,” not merely a pause in our busy week, but the sacred climax of living. He wrote:

>“The Sabbath is not for the sake of the weekdays; the weekdays are for the sake of Sabbath. It is not an interlude but the climax of living.” [^11.1] 

Heschel invites us into sacred time—a weekly rhythm where we meet God not in hurried moments, but in holy rest. 

What does the Sabbath say about your trust? What would your week look like if rest was more than a reward, but worship?

We can’t expect to love God and still look like everyone else. His love demands change, and it even shapes our love for others. 

If the first tablet teaches us to love God above all, the second teaches us to love what He loves—our neighbor.

[^11.1]: Heschel, Abraham Joshua. The Sabbath. Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2005. 

### Love Your Neighbor as Yourself

> Love is more than what we say,  
> It’s how we live from day to day.  
> To love ourselves yet hate our brother,  
> Is to serve the one and scorn the other.

---

> “For the commandments, ‘You shall not commit adultery, You shall not murder, You shall not steal, You shall not covet,’ and any other commandment, are summed up in this word: ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself.’”  
> —Romans 13:9

---

I have three older stepbrothers, but for much of my childhood, I was the only one in the house. That meant a lot of quiet—sometimes comforting, sometimes lonely. I grew up in the ‘80s and ’90s, with books as my closest companions. My mother read to me from infancy, and before long, learning became both my escape and my passion.

I filled the silence with discovery. I still played outside, but I also loved drawing intricate diagrams of the human heart or sketching flowcharts of cellular respiration. I’d pore over my oldest brother’s college textbooks, line by line, until I grasped concepts far beyond my grade. Some would call me a nerd; others would call me a natural-born perfectionist.

Back then, my striving wasn’t unhealthy. It wasn’t about achievement—it was about wonder. I simply loved to learn.

Then, everything changed.

When I was nine, a lot happened _for_ me, but even more happened _to_ me.

I had always excelled in school, especially in art. A teacher recognized my talent and recommended me for a special program at Old Dominion College. Imagine that, college courses while still in elementary school. I balanced it all: art, school, Taekwondo, even baseball. (I was awful at baseball, but I thought my dad liked to watch me play.)

Life was full. Busy, bright, beautiful.

And then came the darkest moment of my life. My friend’s older brother sexually abused me.

I’ll spare the details. But from that day forward, something in me shifted. My passion for excellence mutated into an obsession. I didn’t just want to be smart or strong—I _had_ to be. My inner voice was resolute: I will become so powerful that no one will ever have power over me again.

From the outside, I looked like a driven kid with big dreams. But on the inside, I was fractured, held together by fear and a compulsion to control.

I even changed my name.

My family used to call me Robbie. But after the trauma, I wanted to be called by my first name: James. James sounded strong. Robbie felt feeble.

In my mind, Robbie had failed. He was the one who let those bad things happen. But James demanded respect. James was “perfect.”

And here’s where the commandment, _“Love your neighbor as yourself,”_ becomes more than just a verse; it becomes a mirror.

Because I love James, I built him. He’s everything I _wish I were_: powerful, capable, even imposing. But the weightier question is: Do I love Robbie?

Robbie was the little guy who loved learning, life, art, and being bad at baseball. He was joyful, curious, and innocent. But he was also weak. He got me hurt.

When Scripture calls us to love our neighbor _as ourselves_, it asks a question we often skip: Who is the “self” we’re meant to love?

### Who Do You Love?

If I love my neighbor like I love James, they’ll get the best of me, as long as they are perfect. But nobody is perfect. And whether I like it or not, I have to learn to love Robbie before I can love you. We have to forgive the imperfect parts of ourselves before we can forgive others.

We can practice self-love, but only seeing through the eyes of God allows us to love ourselves as He does.

What does God see in us that makes us worth dying for?

The command to love our neighbor as ourselves is a call to alignment. It invites us into a way of seeing—one that reflects how God sees us.

In this case, the cliché “seeing is believing” may fit. Because sometimes we need someone else to see us before we can see ourselves.

I think back to a few years after my trauma, during one of the lowest points in my life. I was thirteen, tall but awkward, with a beard and severe acne. I was depressed, suicidal, and angry. But then, another art teacher saw something in me.

She saw past my bitter exterior and glimpsed something I couldn’t. Her vision of a different “me” sparked something—a hope for what I could not yet see. That’s what it means to recognize the signature of our Father upon us. When we do, we will agree with His words at creation, that we are “Very good!” (Genesis 1:31)

God’s vision for us is not a perfected state built by human accomplishment, but divine transformation. He is good, and when we reflect Him, we are good.

We are worth loving and we are worth dying for. (John 3:16)

If we embrace that truth for ourselves, we can begin to see others through the same lens. But when we fail to love ourselves, we inevitably fail to love others as well. Our parents are the first “neighbors” we are commanded to love. They are also where we get our initial sense of identity and worth. This is why the Fifth Commandment sits at the intersection of the Two Tablets. It may also be said that how we honor our earthly father reflects how we honor our Heavenly Father.

Before we learn to trust God, we instinctively trust those who raised us. But what happens when that trust is broken? When the father who was meant to protect us instead forges fear? When the mother who should have nurtured us withholds love? When those who were meant to cast a vision of worth and purpose vomit generational hate and violence instead?

The breakdown of the family doesn’t just damage our relationships—it distorts our image of God. If we see our earthly father as cruel, absent, or impossible to please, it becomes difficult to imagine a heavenly Father who is kind, present, and full of grace.

Yet for every child raised in a broken home, I’ve seen another child rebel despite being raised in a loving home. Paul wisely includes _disobedience to parents_ in his chilling list of End-Times sins. [^12.1] To modern eyes, “disobedience to parents” as a grave sin seems absurd. Yet, in God’s eyes, rejecting parental authority is not a small thing. Rejecting family means denying the very institution God ordained as the foundation of community.

Kingdom life begins at home. If love isn’t cultivated in that soil, weeds grow in its place.

**And our fruit will expose us…always.**

### The Test of The Ten

John taught us that the training ground for loving God is found in the trenches of loving each other:

> “If someone says, ‘I love God,’ and hates his brother, he is a liar; for he who does not love his brother whom he has seen, how can he love God whom he has not seen?” (1 John 4:20)

You can appear holy as you lift your hands in worship. A pastor might preach a stirring sermon. A friend might offer a tearful apology.

For just one moment, anyone can look like anything. 

But over time, the truth always emerges.  
You can’t pretend to be patient.  
You can’t conjure kindness.  
You can’t fake forgiveness.

This is where the final five commandments test us—not in public appearances, but in private desires. Whether it’s adultery, murder, theft, or deception, the root always comes deep from within. The final commandments govern not what we do, but what we desire. They speak not to our hands but to our hearts.

Coveting, lust, and discontentment are the silent root of every sin.

Before a man commits adultery, he has already imagined another’s wife as his own.  
Before someone steals, they first resent what they lack.  
Before murder, there is bitterness, jealousy, or the slow poison of indifference.

These commandments teach us one enduring truth: **every external action begins with internal motivation.**

### The First Sin

Adam and Eve had everything. They lived in Paradise. They walked with God, lacking nothing. Yet in that perfect setting, Satan convinced them that God was holding out.

> “Did God really say…?” (Genesis 3:1)

That single question planted a seed of doubt. Doubt gave birth to discontentment. And through that small crack, the door to covetousness swung wide open. Eve suddenly desired what she had never even noticed before. She became convinced that the one thing she needed was the one thing she didn’t have.

> “So when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree desirable to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate.” (Genesis 3:6)

Her temptation followed the same pattern John would later warn us about:

> “For all that is in the world—the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life—is not of the Father but is of the world.” (1 John 2:16)

- **Lust of the flesh** – “Good for food.”
- **Lust of the eyes** – “Pleasant to the eyes.”
- **Pride of life** – “Desirable to make one wise.”

Coveting and lust are always fed by the same lie:  
**God isn’t enough.**

And the fruit of that lie is always the same:  
**Theft. Deception. Destruction.**

It happened in the Garden.  
It happened with King David.  
It happens today.

### The Temptation Trifecta

Coveting doesn’t grow in the light. It flourishes in the shadows—when our souls are vulnerable, empty, or disconnected. That’s why some of the most dangerous spiritual battles happen when we’re tired, bored, and alone. I call this the **Temptation Trifecta**. And there’s no better example than King David.

It’s easy to judge his failure from a distance, as if it came out of nowhere. But sin rarely leaps; it creeps. And by the time David saw Bathsheba, something in him had already gone quiet. It’s also good to remember that by this time, he was already the mighty shepherd king most had come to admire and adore. We shouldn’t read his story with smug pride but with humble dread. For if the Trifecta could seize such a man as he, then who among us can claim to be above him?

David’s story is recorded in 2 Samuel 11–12.

He had been king for years now. The thrill of defeating Goliath was long past. The joy of worshiping in the fields with his harp had faded. The fire that once danced in his prayers was now dimmed by duty and distraction. Perhaps he hadn’t been to the tabernacle in some time. 

It wasn’t that the battles were all won. There were still enemies of Israel making themselves known. But David was just...bored. He had grown weary of the good God had called him to do.

The prophet Samuel, his early mentor and truth-teller, was dead. His dearest friend, Jonathan, was also gone. Iron had stopped sharpening iron, and no one had taken their place. Nathan was a prophet in title, but David had never truly let him in.

And so, in the season when kings went off to war, David lingered at home, wandering on the roof of his palace. [^12.2]

He wasn’t seeking trouble. He wasn’t planning an affair.  
He was just tired, bored, and alone.

Then he saw her. Bathsheba, bathing in the moonlight.  

She was beautiful.  
Forbidden.  
And, in his mind, he deserved her.

David had many wives—one of them, Abigail, was known for both her beauty and her wisdom. He lacked for nothing. But Bathsheba was the **one thing** he didn’t have. And like Eve in the garden, the one thing he didn’t have became the one thing he had to have.

The tragedy unfolded quickly: lust became adultery, adultery became deception, and deception became murder. 

One moment of **lust**. A **lifetime** of consequences.

But thank God for Nathans.

When the prophet confronted David, he played dumb at first. But the Word of God pierced through the fog. And David, broken and ashamed, could only whisper: 

>“I have sinned against the Lord.” (2 Samuel 12:13)

And there was no quick comfort. No pat on the head.  
The child of their union grew sick. And David fasted—face down, day and night—until the boy died.

Then, unexpectedly—  
David rose.  
He washed.  
And he **worshiped**.

His servants were stunned. How could he move from grief to praise so quickly?

Because David wasn’t numbing the pain, he was bringing it back to God. And in the hands of Yahweh, even ashes can grow gardens.

Solomon was born from that same union. And generations later, through him, came Jesus Christ.

Even when we fall, **God is not finished** with us. Our failures are not the final word. There are still chapters to be written.

The commandments written on our hearts are not chains but invitations. They expose the weakness within us, not to condemn us, but to call us home. To lead us in the way everlasting. 

They call us to love God,  
to love ourselves,  
and to love our neighbor.

And that kind of love doesn’t begin with perfection.  
It starts with **surrender**.

Because without surrender, the Spirit has no sail to breathe into, and no rudder to steer the destiny of our hearts.

## Chapter 8: The Power to Live the Law

>Holy words once carved in stone,  
Now blaze within, unseen—but known.  
Not by force, but deep desire—  
Love obeys through holy fire.

---

>“Grace is not opposed to effort. It is opposed to earning. Effort is action. Earning is attitude.” — Dallas Willard [^13.1]

---

The Cross was enough.

Enough to cleanse every sin, cover the deepest iniquity, and crush every chain. What Sinai revealed in smoke, Christ fulfilled in blood. At Pentecost, divine fire moved from heaven into human hearts, rewriting the Law within us, not as cold stone tablets but as flames within living temples surrendered to God.

Paul captures this marvelous truth:

> “For what the law could not do in that it was weak through the flesh, God did by sending His own Son... that the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us who do not walk according to the flesh but according to the Spirit.” (Romans 8:3-4)

Through the Cross, God achieved what human effort never could. Yet, He didn’t merely forgive us—He filled us. He didn’t just wipe the slate clean, He gave us a new heart to write on. 

Now the Spirit empowers righteousness—not by compulsion, but by conviction. Not through striving, but through **transformation**. Ezekiel must have wept as he saw this vision unfold:

> “I will put My Spirit within you and cause you to walk in My statutes, and you will keep My judgments and do them.” (Ezekiel 36:27)

This is the power of God: obedience from the inside out. The Law shifts from external demands to becoming a mirror reflecting who we’re becoming. Paul beautifully describes this inward shift:

> “For I delight in the law of God according to the inward man.” (Romans 7:22)

That word—delight—is profound. Delight defines a renewed heart. The Law, once a burden, becomes a joy. Obedience flows naturally from a heart captivated by Him. 

This is spiritual rebirth.

This is the Mind of God within us.

The Spirit reshapes our desires and renews our minds. He imparts God’s perspective. To be led by the Spirit is to share the very mind of our Creator. And this is the key to continued renewal. (Romans 12:2)

Every daily, intentional, and costly “yes” to the Spirit yields new perspective and new fruit. 

We begin to **see** what God sees.  
We **feel** what He feels.  
We **want** what He wants.

As the prophet Jeremiah foresaw:

> “I will put My law in their minds, and write it on their hearts; and I will be their God, and they shall be My people... For they all shall know Me, from the least of them to the greatest of them.” (Jeremiah 31:33-34)

This is the ultimate fulfillment of the Cross—not His presence from afar, but His Spirit as a constant companion.

## Chapter 9: Missing the Mark - One-Pillar Faith

> To wander far from God’s command,  
> Is missing Him, His perfect plan.  
> The Mark is Christ, both life and light,  
> To miss His love is endless night.  
> Yet grace extends, a bridge of love,  
> To bring us home to Him above.

---

> “Not everyone who says to Me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ shall enter the kingdom of heaven, but he who does the will of My Father in heaven.” (Matthew 7:21)

---

The metaphor “to miss the mark” encapsulates the biblical concept of sin. While this imagery is borrowed from archery, it also suggests that sin is not merely an act of wrongdoing but a failure to attain the Mark of God. In Greek, there are other rich layers to the word “sin,” such as “to wander from the Law of God.” John affirms this second meaning:

> “Whoever commits sin also commits lawlessness, and sin is lawlessness.” (1 John 3:4)

Beyond these definitions, a broader theological theme in Scripture presents sin as a cause of **lost inheritance**. This perspective paints a picture of a destiny forfeited, not merely rebellion or legal violation. The parable of the prodigal son is a vivid illustration of this — a son estranged from his Father, squandering his portion, yet ultimately restored. [^8.1]

Although most people refer to Jesus’s story in Luke 15 as the parable of the prodigal son, I see it as the parable of two sons.

### The Parable of the Two Sons

Dust swirled around Jesus as He stood on a Galilean hillside, His voice rising above the murmurs of two crowds.

One group draws near—notorious sinners, tax collectors, prostitutes, outcasts. Their hearts are eager yet broken. They’d heard whispers of someone both prophet **and** miracle worker, a man who healed with a touch yet spoke like thunder. He held nothing back—neither truth that stung nor grace that healed.

The other group watches more than listens—Pharisees, scribes, temple elites—they stand at a distance, arms crossed, hoping to pounce on the tiniest impropriety or misstep.

He begins a story.

“There was a man who had two sons…”

As the story unfolds, sinners lean in while the religious nervously look away. Jesus isn’t just telling a parable—He’s holding up a mirror to their hearts.

As the passionate rabbi described a son estranged from his Father, lost to even himself, the sinners in the crowd could feel the pain. They knew hopelessness from wandering, the hurt of not belonging. The pious waited in anticipation of judgment, when the lost son would get his due. And their merciless wish was granted.

Jesus continued—painting the wreckage of rebellion: a squandered inheritance, an empty soul, a belly aching with famine. And then came the lowest moment: starving on hands and knees amongst the most unclean of animals.

The sinners could relate. They knew the stench of mud and pig excrement. Even as Jesus spoke, their stomachs growled. And their hearts, like those of the lost son, ached for the Father’s embrace to fill the void of their soul.

But Jesus kept the story of the lost son going. A son not truly lost but actually found—and not just found but welcomed. In this story, the son of sin is received back by the Father, not with disgust but compassion. The fatted calf is prepared, and the entire community of the Father’s house rejoices as heaven rejoices with a hundred million angels strong. Well, almost everyone in the Father’s house celebrates…

The sinners were swept into the joy of restoration. Could it be true—for them? But the religious weren’t done listening. They remembered. This was a story of _two_ sons. Their frowns deepened. “Here comes the punch,” they thought.

Jesus turns His gaze to the teachers of the law, bringing His lesson to its piercing conclusion. And so He tells them of the second son, faithful in deeds but far from the Father. A grand celebration for one son revealed the jealousy and aloofness of the other. Through stubborn pride, the older son refused to enter the Father’s house. Though his Father pleaded, he rejected fellowship. He remained outside, a symbol of missing the kingdom of God entirely.

So the story ends—not with a conclusion, but with a question:  
Will the older son ever come inside?

He had faithfulness, but not fellowship. He lived in the house… but did he ever know the Father?

This narrative illustrates the fullest picture of sin, embodying the essence of missing the mark, wandering from God’s Law, and being without a share in kingdom inheritance. The heart of this story is about a relationship with the Father. To miss that is to miss everything.

The parable of the two sons echoes a story of two sisters. The older brother and Martha are a sobering warning: we can be in the Father’s house and still be far from His heart.

### Martha Missed the Mark

In the quiet town of Bethany, we find Mary and Martha. Two sisters welcomed Jesus into their home—but only one truly made room for Him. Their story in Luke 10 shows how even good intentions can distract us from what matters most.

Martha, likely the elder sister, was energetic, pragmatic, and well-respected in her village. As Jesus approached her home, she opened the door wide, sweeping away the last bit of dust from the threshold and welcoming Him with a smile.

When Jesus and His disciples were seated, she dashed off to the kitchen. Pots clanged, and the smell of fresh bread filled the house. Martha was a great host. But her focus soon drifted from the One who dwelled in her home—though perhaps not yet in her heart.

In total contrast, Mary chooses to sit at Jesus’s feet, embracing the posture of a disciple, intently absorbing every word He utters. Although Martha’s house was well prepared, her heart was distracted. And it truly showed as she stopped suddenly, realizing she was alone in the kitchen.

_Where was Mary?_ she thought with a tinge of fury.

She came around the corner, and Mary was sitting on the floor by Jesus. Now the tinge of fury became more pronounced. She stood at the kitchen door tapping her foot louder and louder, expecting Jesus to notice the situation and tell Mary to do her part.

But He didn’t notice at all. He was telling a light-hearted story to pass the time until supper—or so it seemed to Martha. His disciples chuckled. Then Mary snorted as she laughed, which made Jesus erupt in laughter. He smiled brightly and leaned toward Mary as she laughed, clearly enjoying her delight.

And that was the final straw for Martha:

> “Lord, do You not care that my sister has left me to serve alone? Therefore tell her to help me.” (Luke 10:40)

The room became uncomfortably quiet. It was usually Jesus’s words that made the crowd go silent. He looked up slowly at Martha, her disgust apparent from the crooked scowl on her face.

“Martha,” He said sternly and a bit disappointed.

“You are worried and troubled about many things,” He paused to ensure she was listening.

“Only one thing is needed...and Mary has chosen that good thing.” [^8.2]

Then He added, “And it will not be taken from her.” Jesus’s tone sounded firm and final. Martha dared not push back.

Like the older son in the previous parable, Martha served well. She had the same access to Jesus as Mary, but Martha missed the Mark. She was so busy serving Him that she missed Him. And that is vital because **Jesus is the Mark.** Jesus is the Tav—the final letter, the eternal Mark. [^8.3]

When read together, these stories reveal the heart of the Gospel: God is not seeking servants who merely obey commands, but children who know Him, love Him, and sit at His feet. Perhaps you can relate to some of these characters. Maybe you’re asking yourself whether your life reflects Mary’s devotion or Martha’s distraction.

Perhaps, like the lost son, you’re pretending to be satisfied with a life that’s actually destroying you. Or like the older son, maybe you’re devoted to “good” things while keeping God at a distance.

While we all might stray, there’s an even greater danger—believing we’ve hit the Mark because of what we do for God, while never truly knowing Him. The Mark of God is not just about right doctrine or right behavior. These stories warn us: embrace one of the Two Pillars while ignoring the other, and you will still miss Him.

These are the “Super Christians” who will miss the Mark.

### Super Christians Who Will Miss the Mark

There are two senses in which one might sin or miss the mark. In the most obvious sense, we all have sinned, and our righteousness is as “filthy rags” (Isaiah 64:6). Romans 3:23 succinctly states, “For all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God.” This implies that despite our best efforts, all humans rebel against God. We are all the prodigal son and must repent to be redeemed.

Yet there is another more disturbing example of what it means to miss the Mark of God. Many stories in the Bible stand as enduring cautionary tales for those who are alive to read them. Jesus Himself warns of a Day when a multitude who believe they are secure in their salvation will face a harsh reality. He prophesied:

> “Many will say to Me in that day, ‘Lord, Lord, have we not prophesied in Your name, cast out demons in Your name, and done many wonders in Your name?’ And then I will declare to them, ‘I never knew you; depart from Me, you who practice lawlessness!’” (Matthew 7:22–23)

These are the ones who clung to the Second Pillar, but ignored the First. They built ministries without intimacy, and platforms without prayer. They obeyed commands, yet never knew the Commander. To miss one pillar, is to miss the Mark completely.

This prophecy underscores that mere acknowledgment of Jesus’s lordship or the performance of deeds in His name is insufficient for redemption. It’s a sobering reminder that eternal life is not promised to those who possess an external facade of righteousness, no matter how respected and polished it may appear.

Notice that Jesus calls out two qualities lacking in these **super**ficial Christians. First, He says, “I never knew you.” Jesus is highlighting that He has no relationship with them. Let that sink in—they will profess Jesus and perform miracles in His name, yet He will say: “I never knew you.” 

Then He pronounces judgment upon these people by saying, “Depart from Me, you who practice lawlessness!” This is critical, because it highlights what I will cover in detail in the second part of this book. The most damning and dangerous people in the End Times will not be atheists or agnostics, they will be wolves in sheep’s clothing, proclaiming Christ as Lord, but rejecting Him as King. And they’ll **practice** lawlessness, and likely teach others that breaking God’s Law carries no consequence. (Matthew 5:19)

This is not a proverb, this is prophecy!

One final, chilling detail: Jesus said ‘Many’—not just a few who slip through the cracks.

**To miss the Mark of God is not just to fail morally but to forfeit eternal life.**

We often imagine the Mark as a future crisis. But its **spirit is already active**. 

When we hear “Antichrist,” we often picture a future tyrant or sinister figure bent on global domination. But Scripture teaches that the **spirit of the Antichrist** is not coming—it is **already here**.

### Victor Whiskey

Some believe they’ll rise to the occasion in the moment of testing. That when tribulation comes, courage will somehow appear. They will choose God when it matters most—even if they’ve neglected Him in everything leading up to it.

**God’s mark is forged long before the fire.**

Consider the crucible of **Delta Force Selection**.

Delta Force is the elite of the elite—an ultra-secretive special operations unit that accepts only the most exceptional soldiers in the U.S. military.

The candidates are stripped of rank, name, and comfort. For weeks, they are pushed to the edge of physical and psychological endurance—alone, in silence, navigating impossible terrain with only a compass, a rucksack, and time. There is no encouragement, no praise, just the voice in their head—and the weight on their back.

Eric Haney, a founding member of Delta Force, described men breaking down in tears mid-march. Some collapsed from exhaustion; others stepped off the path and whispered, “I’m done.”

A candidate doesn’t scream or throw his gear down when he quits. He walks into the tent and speaks two words: **Victor Whiskey**—voluntary withdrawal. And in that moment, it’s over.

Inside, they are given warmth, coffee, and a hot meal. A sense of relief washes over them.

But then, the regret sets in.

Some try to go back. They plead with instructors, claiming they made a mistake. But the answer is always the same:

> “You can change your mind. But you’ve already shown us your heart.” [^5]

There is no second chance. 

Because the Selection isn’t about who’s strong enough—it’s about who’s ready before the test even begins.

So it will be as the days of tribulation approach.

When the final test descends—when the Mark of the Beast is demanded, when allegiance costs everything—you will not suddenly become faithful. You will only become **who you already are**.

The fire will not give you strength.

The fire comes only to **remove or refine**.

If in your heart is the **gold of humility**, the **silver of faithfulness**, the fire will purify it.

But if your heart harbors the **chaff of compromise** or the **stubble of complacency**, the fire will consume. Only ash will remain.

>“For behold, the day is coming, Burning like an oven, And all the proud, yes, all who do wickedly will be stubble. And the day which is coming shall burn them up...” (Malachi 4:1)

But for those who fear the Lord, the fire does not consume—it refines like silver, it purifies like gold (Malachi 3:2–3).

I can’t bear the thought of being unprepared as that Day approaches—like a thief in the night, it comes. And I wake, only to find my faith is made of fodder. That gut-check moment when the image I built of myself collapses under the weight of truth.

Not tested.
Not refined.
Just burned away.

It’s this:
**What Mark do we bear right now?**

### The Birthplace of Compromise

The Roman Empire was not a neutral backdrop for the rise of Christianity—it was a crucible of conformity and cultural syncretism. The early Church’s drift is best understood when contrasted with the moral rot of the empire in which it tried to survive. 

To be fair, Rome’s genius was real—the roads, law, language, and architecture. Its innovation and ingenuity were matched only by its innovations for death and its craving for immorality: the arenas, crucifixion, licentiousness, and emperor worship. 

Emperor Tiberius retired to Capri, where, according to Suetonius, he maintained a villa of horrors involving unspeakable acts with children and slaves. Sex shows, temple prostitution, and orgiastic feasts were available for public display in Roman society. Human life was expendable. Spectacles of death, featuring criminals, slaves, and Christians, were routine entertainment.

Emperor Constantine imprinted his face on currency next to Sol Invictus, the Unconquered Sun, while his mother popularized relic worship. [^14.3] [^14.4] Rome celebrated sin while Nero lit Christians on fire like candles to light his garden at night. What our modern society does in secret, the elite of Rome and Alexandria did in the open. These were twin cities of moral depravity. The influence of these cultures pressed like a tsunami into the world abroad. Hardly the kind of society that should be trusted with the sacred protection of the Christian faith. 

Alexandria became a hub for hybrid religion. Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria merged Greek philosophy with Scripture, introducing Platonism. Later Church fathers like Origen adopted this philosophy into the interpretation of Scripture, diluting the rich spiritual practices of the Church into mere rhetorical symbolism. This kind of thinking eventually led to abandoning literal obedience to the Sabbath and Passover.

This imperial alliance didn’t just alter worship—it birthed practices that defy the Mark of God, the sign of allegiance to His authority. The Roman Catholic Church, heir to this empire-church fusion, enshrined doctrines that rebel against Scripture, the teachings of Jesus, and the Apostles. Even after the Reformation, most Protestant churches still echo these same deviations.

But not all bowed the knee or kissed the ring. There has always been a faithful Remnant to resist the tide of corruption. Those who were willing to build on nothing less than Christ and the teachings He passed down to His disciples. Those are the foundations of our faith—and we have lost them!

We forgot what God commanded us to remember: His holy days, His Passover, His appointed times, and filled the void with pagan holidays dressed in Christian names.

**2. Lost Sabbath** - Trading His Rest for Endless Striving
The blessing of the seventh day was outlawed and replaced, robbing us of the rhythm and rest God wove into Creation itself.

**3. Lost Authority** - Trading His Word for Human Hierarchy
We stopped testing everything against Scripture and the Spirit, handing over our discernment to popes and mega-church pastors.

**4. Lost Treasure** - Trading the Poor for Palaces
We inverted God’s design, pouring resources into buildings and salaries while the broken and forgotten received the leftovers.

**5. Lost Love** - Trading Intimacy for Religion
We abandoned our first love, trading the intimacy of the Secret Place for buffet cultural Christianity.

**6. Lost Power** - Trading the Spirit for Performance
We abandoned fasting and the baptism of the Holy Spirit, leaving us with a form of godliness but no power to heal the sick or hear His voice.

**7. Lost Mission** - Trading Disciples for Attendees
We stopped building disciples and started building empires. We’ve lost our purpose and our mission.

**This progression shows that each foundation doesn’t just fall; it causes the next to crumble.**

This slow but progressive erosion reflects the same growing abomination upon abomination of Ezekiel 8. Ezekiel saw that Israel’s sin was so severe that it drove God from His own sanctuary. What a terrible consequence! It’s a warning: He will remove the light of His Presence even from His sacred Temple. No church is immune.

And just like in Ezekiel’s time, ruin didn’t happen overnight, but over long periods of moral decay. The people still claimed to worship Yahweh, but their devotion was divided. Their praise turned hollow.

To modern readers, the abominations seen in his vision feel distant—some bygone sin of the past. We shake our heads at ancient idolatry, confident we’d never bow to the sun or worship another god. But I submit that Israel didn’t feel they were doing those things either. Yet there is no denying that the practices of their faith morphed into acts and traditions so grotesque they broke the heart of God.

Their traditions grew into something that directly opposed the will of God.

What if we’ve grown numb to sin that offends God, just like Israel did?

What if the symbols and rhythms of our worship resemble more pagan practice than godly glory?

Listen to what God asks Ezekiel:

> “Have you seen this, O son of man? Is it a trivial thing... to commit the abominations which they commit here?” (Ezekiel 8:17)

To Israel, it felt trivial; to God, it was an abomination.

That’s why God marks the mourners: they still feel. Do we still feel? It’s a question worth asking. There is evidence that we have become much like Israel in those days. That’s what we will explore in the coming chapters. And let us not forget that those things were written as warnings to us today:

>“Now all these things happened to them as examples, and they were written for our admonition, on whom the ends of the ages have come.”  (1 Corinthians 10:11)

And so these chapters confront the question:

What if the mixed worship of a few has influenced the many?

Ezekiel’s vision wasn’t about **all** of Israel falling; it exemplified **two** distinct groups: one following corrupt leaders, the other crying, “Enough!” In Ezekiel 9, God commands His angelic messenger to mark those who sigh and cry over the abominations. As we’ve explored, this mark was a sign of those who shared God’s grief over sin—those who refused to grow numb. 

If Ezekiel’s vision teaches us anything, it’s that God takes worship seriously. We must decide who we are: the compromised and comfortable or the grieved and bereaved.

If the foundations of our faith are broken, are we willing to change? There is hope. We can change. These foundations can be restored. Removing the diseased parts can be painful for most, but it is possible. Let us begin with our Lost Memory.

## Chapter 14: Lost Memory

> Traditions rise, yet truth grows dim—  
> We’ve kept the feast, but not for Him.
> We decorate, we dance and sing,  
> But miss His day of suffering.
> The days He gave, we’ve cast aside—  
> And wonder why His fire’s died.

---

> “Do this in remembrance of Me.” (Luke 22:19)

---

“Do this in remembrance of Me,” Christ said on the eve of His crucifixion.

He asked us to remember—His body broken, His blood poured out. The perfect sacrifice of the spotless Lamb. In the same chapter where Paul expounded the Lord’s Supper, he praised the believers because they **remembered**:

> “Now I praise you that you _remember_ me in all things and _keep the traditions just as I delivered them to you_.” (1 Corinthians 11:2, emphases added)

But that praise had a pivot. The very next words in Paul’s letter shift from commendation to correction:

> “In giving these instructions, I do not praise you, since you come together not for the better but for the worse.” (1 Corinthians 11:17)

And what was that correction?

They had forgotten the heart of Passover—the feast of remembrance. They turned Holy Communion into a common get-together. They forgot what the day was meant to remember.

It raises the question: **Have our traditions lost their way?**

I say these things as someone who’s been just as guilty. I get the struggle. I’ve lived it. Traditions can be so good, they knit us together. That’s why they matter: they unite us. But the question remains—**united to whom?**

Would Paul praise us for the rituals we hold so dear? Or would he plead with us to return to what’s been lost?

### The Sign of Our Worship

But these holy days are not just reminders—they are worship. Passover is worship. The Sabbath is worship. The Feasts are worship. God established seven annual feasts. Each one is a prophetic rehearsal, a sacred appointment that tells the story of salvation from beginning to end. [^15.1] 

But they are more than worship. They are also signs. They are part of the Mark of God, visible signs of a people set apart for Him. A people who remember their God and are remembered by Him. [^15.2] But to mix holy signs with pagan symbols blurs that Mark of allegiance. And many of us have done exactly that.

### Mixed Worship

When Israel made the golden calf in the wilderness, they didn’t call it Ba’al; they called it Yahweh. They called it a feast “to Yahweh.” (Exodus 32:5) They sang. They celebrated. They remembered their deliverance from Egypt. But their worship was mixed, with culture, comfort, and compromise. God didn’t say, “It’s the thought that counts.” 

He called it an abomination.

We may not have golden calves anymore, but we’ve still got mixed worship. We combine God’s name with seasons He never sanctified, and traditions that still nod to their idol beginnings.

We don’t call it rebellion. We call it Easter and hunt for eggs from a fertile little bunny.

We don’t call it unholy. We call it All Hallows‘ Eve, a time defined by the symbols of Samhain.

But what would He call it?

**Let me pause.** I know this stirs strong feelings—I’ve felt them. I’ve had these conversations in person, and the pushback is palpable. I’m not trying to spoil anyone’s childhood. I have fond memories of holidays like Christmas and Easter, too. And honestly, there’s room to enjoy seasons like winter: decorate with evergreen, make it “snow” in your living room, kindle a fire, and sing about peace on earth.

In the fall, feel free to make wreaths of leaves, sip a latte, or carve a pumpkin with a goofy grin. But on nights heavy with dark mystique, let your porch point to Christ with quiet love and simple welcome. Enjoy the beauty of each season, but let the holy days tell a holy story. If some customs have tangled roots, it’s better to prune them than indulge them.

This isn’t about killing joy or becoming a religious weirdo. It’s not about erasing cherished memories. It’s about intentionally creating better ones—the kind that honor the Most High in the way He asked us to.

To humbly obey His voice.
To add nothing.
To take nothing.

But like Israel, and so many today, what may have begun with holy intentions has become _strange fire_ (Leviticus 10:1-2). Are we guilty of celebrating days God never commanded? Or worse, mixing idol worship with days He did command?

How many praise the resurrection with symbols of fertility, and serve it with the flesh of swine? How many sing of a Savior born in Bethlehem and then tell stories of an Odin-like Santa in the sky?

We make it rain candy on Halloween and dress ourselves to blend in with the spirits that roam. We leave offerings and lanterns on our porch, lighting a path for darkness to our door.

These pagan rites are also worship, **but for demons.** [^15.3]

“Holiday” comes from the word “holy day.” So why do we cling so tightly to holidays with such unholy roots while ignoring the _Mo’edim Q’doshim_, the holy days of Yahweh? In doing so, we’ve replaced the signs of the living God with the symbols of false gods.

I’m not casting guilt. I’m trying to stir honest reflection.

Somewhere along the way, the Church stopped remembering and started replacing. But rather than honor what He created, we made room for what was convenient. Something familiar to the world, but foreign to the Light of the World.

Paul’s questions to the Corinthians still echo today:

> “What fellowship has righteousness with lawlessness? And what communion has light with darkness? And what accord has Christ with Belial? Or what part has a believer with an unbeliever? And what agreement has the temple of God with idols? For you are the temple of the living God. As God has said: ‘I will dwell in them and walk among them. I will be their God, and they shall be My people.’ Therefore, ‘Come out from among them and be separate, says the Lord. Do not touch what is unclean, and I will receive you.’” (2 Corinthians 6:14-17)

### A Frog and a Diet Coke

Consider it like this: imagine if I treated my marriage the way many of us have treated God’s holy days.

I have been happily married for over 20 years. One small way I keep my marriage strong is by writing little love notes to my wife. Each morning, I make her coffee fresh and ready before she rises. On occasion, I also leave a note by her favorite coffee mug. This daily act reminds her of how much I adore her.

One morning, I left her a note with a sketch of a sea otter. It’s a special nickname between us. She’s playful, full of life, and, if I may say so, adorable. Her favorite mug says, _“What the Elf?!”_ If you know her, you know how fitting that is. And, of course, the coffee is made just how she likes it, something I’ve fine-tuned over two decades.

But imagine one day she walks downstairs expecting that same loving ritual, and instead, finds yesterday’s stale coffee in the pot. There’s a note, but it’s not what she’s expecting. It has a frog on it. And instead of her favorite mug, a Diet Coke is waiting on the counter. To be clear, this latter scenario is entirely hypothetical, but stay with me because it’s essential.

My wife isn’t a fan of frogs. In fact, I’m pretty sure they repulse her. But do you know who loves frogs? My ex-girlfriend. And the Diet Coke? You guessed it, the ex-girlfriend. 

Now, how do you think my wife would respond?

Let’s envision that after she discovers this scene, I come cheerfully down the stairs, expecting her to be delighted. But instead, she’s hurt. Really hurt. Her eyes tear up. 

I realize what’s wrong, so I reach for her hand. I look her in the eyes. And I tell her, “These symbols are meant for you. They have nothing to do with my ex-girlfriend. I happen to like frogs and Diet Coke more than sea otters and coffee. But this is all for you.”

Really—do you think that would fly?

Even if you don’t know my wife, you know this: no self-respecting woman would accept that kind of “love.”

If we can see clearly how painful and absurd that scenario would be for my wife, can we honestly believe God would feel any less betrayed by our mixing of pagan idolatry into worship meant solely for Him?

Looking at the evidence, it seems we’ve forgotten who we are. It seems we have also forgotten **whose** we are.

God is not some abstract deity, indifferent to how we love Him. He is a *jealous* God, not in a petty way, but with a pure, holy love that deserves fidelity. My wife deserves a love that’s as special and unique as she is. If that’s true for her, how much more does God deserve our undivided devotion?

### The Holy Days of Yahweh

When God formed the heavenly bodies, before He said they were for light upon the earth, He said they were for “signs and seasons.” [^15.5]
The holy days of Yahweh are His calendar set in motion from “the beginning”. 

But unlike our calendar, God’s calendar isn’t built on productivity and profit. It’s built on remembrance and redemption. He gave us appointed times to retell His story and to mark His people. 

> “These are the Lord’s appointed feasts…a statute forever throughout your generations.” (Leviticus 23:37,41)

The **Spring Feasts** celebrate redemption and renewal (Passover, Unleavened Bread, and Pentecost), and the **Fall Feasts** point toward judgment and, ultimately, restoration (Trumpets, Atonement, Tabernacles, and The Last Great Day). 

These aren’t random holidays.  
They are rehearsals.  
Prophetic appointments.  
Holy shadows of something yet to come. (Colossians 2:17)

Jesus fulfilled the meaning of the spring feasts perfectly in His first coming, down to the very hour. And God commands these rhythms to keep us grounded in His story, the Gospel. One of the early defenders of God’s Holy Days, Polycrates, believed them to be a part of the very Gospel handed down by the Apostles. [^15.4]

We remember important moments, such as anniversaries and birthdays, not to relive the past but to honor their significance. We do so to ensure we don’t forget what matters most.

To forget an anniversary is to neglect a covenant.  
To miss a wedding day is to forsake our dearest love.

The **Spring Feasts** are like an anniversary. These are days to remember what happened—when love poured out, sin was buried, and the Spirit came to make us new.

The **Fall Feasts**, in contrast, are more like a **wedding rehearsal**. They look ahead to something yet to come. They’re like the parable of the ten virgins, reminding us to be awake, alert, and ready with oil in our lamps because the Bridegroom is near.

These seven holy days and the Sabbath are not legal obligations—they are _invitations_. Our salvation does not rest on observing the feasts in any particular way, but we certainly aren’t commanded to honor Halloween. In fact, we are not commanded to remember Christ’s birth or even His resurrection; but we are commanded to remember His death until He comes again (1 Corinthians 11:26). We are free to observe these holy days in ways that move our hearts [^15.8], but we are expected to remember rightly. [^15.7] Paul warns how we remember the Lord’s Supper is a matter of life and death. The Lord’s Supper (Communion) and the Lord’s Passover are one and the same. [^15.9]

Remembering Communion as Passover has transformed this sacrament from a forced moment to a sacred supper. Every year, Passover arrives with increasing richness. It is truly holy! Sitting at the table with the same symbols remembered for over three thousand years is powerful and cathartic. 

Celebrating Trumpets brings a fierce but loving urgency to our church. We celebrate with homemade trumpet piñatas for children to play with. We reteach and relive the symbols of His Second Coming. These are the “Days of Awe,” and they are not forgotten in our home or in our hearts. [^15.1]

For more than a millennium before Christ, Israel remembered these sacred times; for two millennia since, a faithful remnant has remembered them as well.

### It’s Time to Remember

I’m not just trying to throw shade on familiar holidays. I’m trying to honor His **holy days**. It’s a sad fact, but the passing of Passover began a cascade of compromise. [^15.6] What might have appeared as a controversy of calendars was something far more dangerous: a pattern of abandoning what God commands us to remember.

Jesus said, “Do this in remembrance of Me...” and we have forgotten. 

Yahweh proclaimed, “Remember the Sabbath day...” and it has been forsaken. The voice of God thundered from Mount Sinai to all who stood there: to Moses, the Israelites, and to the Egyptians who came with them.

> “Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy.” (Exodus 20:8)

This word, “Remember,” is not a polite suggestion. It is a command. A warning. A plea. Because He knew. He knew how quickly we would forget. He knew remembering would take brave intention. And friends, we have a lot to remember, in the light of our great forgetting.

What’s lost when we forget what God compels us to remember?

A Church that forgets is a Church that forsakes.
A Church that conforms is a Church that compromises.
And a Church that drifts is a Church ripe to be deceived.

The next foundation to fold was the sacred day of rest. Blessed by God, but rejected by many.

## Chapter 15: Lost Sabbath

>A day of rest, by God’s own hand,
The seventh sealed, a firm command.
Yet Rome pushed back, the Sun held sway,
And Sabbath faded, lost today.

---

>“Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy. Six days you shall labor and do all your work, but the seventh day is the Sabbath of the Lord your God...” (Exodus 20:8–10)

---

Picture this: a gift from God, wrapped at Creation, blessed forever. Then imagine it twisted, shoved aside, and forsaken. That’s the Sabbath. A holy day, handcrafted by God, yet somehow buried under centuries of compromise.

In the previous chapter, we talked about the lost memory of God’s holy days, but the Sabbath is the ultimate consequence of our amnesia. It’s not just a holy day, it’s **the** holy day. Unlike the sabbaths tied to the Feasts, the weekly Sabbath predates Sinai and is one of the Ten Commandments. How terribly ironic that it is the one commandment we have most forgotten, and it begins with the word “Remember.”

Let me clarify: I’m not saying Sunday worship is a sin. I’m not saying you’re doomed if you don’t gather on Saturday. Keeping the Sabbath holy matters; that’s why Jesus and the disciples gathered on the Sabbath, but the heart of this command is **ceasing**.

And that’s what hit me the hardest. Stopping. 

I shared briefly in Chapter 1 when the weight of the Sabbath sank in for me. It wasn’t a calm moment. My cheeks burned. My pulse kicked. Honestly, it freaked me out. If this were real, if God really meant it, it meant I had been missing something big. 

Questions flooded me: _How do I even do this? What will my church friends think? What’s my wife going to say!?_ Yet beneath the panic, something steadier rose—purpose and connection.

As I began to make this day holy, I felt a sense of renewal in my relationship with God for the first time in years. Like estranged friends finally talking again, I began to reconnect with Him in an ancient and visceral way.

And that’s because this day is ancient and its power is primal. 

### The Power of a Blessed Day

Genesis 2 suggests a beautiful idea: whatever God blesses, He empowers to bear fruit and multiply. The last thing He blessed was a day, the seventh day. God built it into Creation to multiply life, not limit it. Don’t miss this: what God blesses, He multiplies. When God blessed the animals, they multiplied. When God blessed Adam and Eve, He said, “Be fruitful and multiply.” And that blessing hasn’t expired. However, this blessing is only unlocked when we enter into it with intentional devotion to Him.

So, what does this blessing look like in real life?

My oldest daughter, Maddie, is living proof. She stands out at her Christian college, not for having more Bible knowledge, but for ceasing on Saturday. No homework, no hustle, just rest and worship. Her friends, who also love Jesus, call it radical. Wise, even. They see something in her that they don’t have. She’s not just following a rule; she’s partaking in a divine gift that forces her to be still.

What’s most impressive is that she **is** highly accomplished, and yet she remains untethered from the frenetic need for more. Sure, she feels stress or anxiety like everyone else, one of the less helpful propensities I passed on, but most importantly, she is at peace. She doesn’t let the temptation to strive enslave her. One of the most potent ways she guards her heart is by guarding the sacred Sabbath.

At **6 p.m.** every Friday, the books and laptop close, and so do her eyes. No matter the pressure or looming exam, she ceases. There is a trailing voice nagging for just a little bit more, but instead she breathes in deep. She holds a moment as her heart rate tumbles down. Then, steady and calm, she exhales the last remaining worries of the week. At last, a smile adorns her face as she quietly whispers, “Thank you Father.”

That practice allows her to enter the supernatural rest of Yahweh. This is her rhythm because this is His rhythm. And she adores slowing down with Jesus.

John Mark Comer captures this beautifully in The Ruthless Elimination of Hurry:

>“The Sabbath is like a governor on the speed of life.” [^1]

The Sabbath commandment may be seen as a spiritual discipline, but it is also a divine appointment of the highest order. God built it, blessed it, and then commanded it. Like a weekly anniversary, it is a holy time set apart to commence a holy meeting. Entering into that hallowed space changes us. Its effect pours into all the available corners of our hearts like oil filling an empty vat.

Walter Brueggemann, someone Comer and I both owe tribute to, has said:

>“People who keep Sabbath live all seven days differently.” [^2]

You can’t come slamming into Friday night—having exhausted yourself all week—and expect to be still. You have to prepare, to anticipate, to regulate your life to approach the Sabbath with the awe and reverence He deserves. But once you do, I know you will find it as liberating and life-giving as I do. The change may be small at first, but like plants seeking the sun, we lean more and more heavily toward the source of our life, until we are perfectly positioned to drink from its rays. Jesus extends an eternal invitation:

>“Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take My yoke upon you and learn from Me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls.“ (Matthew 11:28—29)

Many don’t notice Jesus echoes Jeremiah’s promise of ”rest for your souls“ (Jeremiah 6:16, 18:15). He invites us into God’s ancient way—one that includes the Sabbath rhythm God gave His people. The invitation is for us to find rest for our souls, but the warning is that ”My people have forgotten Me.“ And that what the Sabbath is all about, Him. And that’s what Jesus was trying to teach in Matthew 11.

Jesus‘ words are spoken in the context of restoring the true meaning of the Sabbath, not abolishing it. And He was not watering down the Sabbath to a mere symbolic rest either. Rather, He called us to rebel against man’s authority and accept His. He is Lord of the Sabbath, not religious rulers, not emperors, Him.

Whose authority have you accepted regarding the Sabbath?

### Join the Resistance

The Sabbath is a bold declaration of faith. It resists the tyranny of busyness and proclaims that our identity is found in God, not in what we accomplish. Brueggemann’s work, which I cited, is titled _Sabbath as Resistance_. It is resistance, but not against God’s people. It’s resistance against every deadline, pharaoh, and empire that demands more without regard for your soul. It’s rebellion against a world that is never satisfied, never content.

But I won’t pretend that keeping the fourth commandment won’t cost you something; it should. It will feel inconvenient and, at times, unpopular. Although many claim the Sabbath is resistance, there are still others who resist the Sabbath. So which resistance will you join?

Is the Sabbath still holy—and does God still linger to await our pause on the seventh day? 

If so, does the way we honor the Sabbath stand out to the world? Is it a ”sign“ like Exodus 31 proclaims it should be?

If the Sabbath isn’t holy anymore, that begs the following question: who changed it? 

Who altered the command God wrote with His own finger and the day He fashioned by His own hand? 

Jesus didn’t. The apostles didn’t. But history hints that Rome did. Despite popular belief, most of the early Church did not abandon the Sabbath. Even into the fifth century, most churches kept the Sabbath, while Rome and Alexandria were the notable exceptions. [^3] 

I know some of you will still have questions about different Bible verses or writings of early Christians. I had those questions too. That’s why I’ve included Appendix E to shed light on these topics. But after much study, it’s clear to me that most have us have inherited a tainted message. 

The Sabbath says _no_ to the world and _yes_ to Him. And yet, commanded or not commanded, why would you say _no_ to the Sabbath?

History warns us: when we reject what God sets apart, we erode the foundation of His authority. And that’s precisely what happened next.

## Chapter 16: Lost Authority

> A little horn, pompous and proud,  
> He sits as God, lawless and loud.  
> He lifts a cup of faithless lies,
> While saints beneath him mercy cry.  
> O Church, awake—seek Christ alone,  
> Flee from his throne, return to home.

> “He shall speak words against the Most High,  
> Shall persecute the saints of the Most High,  
> And shall intend to change times and law.”  
> (Daniel 7:25)

First, let me clarify that this chapter is not meant to elevate man and browbeat you into submission to your pastor or priest; quite the opposite. It’s about something far more dangerous, and yet far more liberating.

Throughout the Church’s history, authority has been stolen, redirected, and hijacked by men who claimed to speak for God while silencing His voice. The question isn’t academic: **Who has the right to command our life and worship—God or man?**

Our answer to that question influences everything: how we worship, when we worship, and what we believe is true. But here’s the good news: there is a standard by which all authority can be tested. We are not at the mercy of any one person to dictate absolute truth. We have His Word and, most importantly, we have His Spirit. True authority rests on the Scripture and the Spirit, confirmed through a plurality of Spirit-filled believers.

### The Test of Authority: Scripture and the Spirit

All believers have access to spiritual understanding through the Holy Spirit (John 14:26, 1 John 2:20, 27). The same Spirit who inspired Scripture helps us discern its meaning. He is the author of the Book, after all. And this is a good thing, because it doesn’t replace teachers; it empowers them. It provides a shared witness that all can appeal to, allowing us to say together “yes” and “amen.”

This principle is perfectly illustrated in the story of the Bereans. When the Apostle Paul and Silas came to Berea, Paul was already famous, highly educated, multilingual, and the most qualified teacher of his time. It makes perfect sense that in Acts 17:11, it says that those in Berea “received the word with all readiness.” 

However, it also says that they “searched the Scriptures daily to find out whether these things were so.” Can you imagine that?! They didn’t just take Paul at his word. They tested everything against the only standard that mattered—God’s Word.

However, God’s Word can be handed down both in writing and by word of mouth. Some principles and practices of today are not explicitly written in Scripture, though they may be consistent with it. Unwritten traditions have a place in the Church, but we must be careful in deciding which traditions pass the test of authentic authority.

God has said, “Do not commit adultery,” yet if someone teaches that adultery is acceptable, or even good, would any title or tradition make such a message valid? Of course not. If there is a conflict between God’s word and man’s, it should be obvious who wins. Yet history shows that even the Church has defied His commands.

### The Role of Tradition: A Double-Edged Sword

Tradition, rightly tested, can preserve the truth. It can strengthen our community with God and neighbor. The danger only arises when tradition begins to speak *over* Scripture rather than *echo* it.

Traditions can be both beneficial and extra-biblical. For clarity: by _extra-biblical_, I mean customs not found in Scripture but consistent with its teaching. By _unbiblical_, I mean those that contradict or override God’s Word.

Consider the synagogue: Although not commanded in the Bible, synagogues emerged during the Babylonian exile when the Temple was inaccessible. God’s people gathered for prayer and Scripture reading (Ezekiel 14:1; 20:1), a tradition upheld by Jesus and the Apostles (Luke 4:16). Another example is the Feast of Dedication (Hanukkah), a post-biblical observance Jesus Himself attended (John 10:22). These traditions are _extra-biblical_, yet not _unbiblical_. They arise from a desire to honor God and reinforce biblical truth. 

Other customs developed in the early Church and were perpetuated by the apostles, such as meeting in homes for worship (Acts 2:46). There may also have been different practices or teachings that were not recorded in Scripture. Consider the practice of fasting before baptism, or fasting for one’s enemies, as described in the _Didache_.[^1] These practices do not contradict the written Word; they build on it. This may have been why Paul said, “Hold the traditions which you were taught, whether by word or our epistle.” Apostolic traditions were both written and spoken (2 Thessalonians 2:15). 

Other possible examples include remembering the birth or resurrection of Jesus. These memorials honor central truths of the faith, provided they are not celebrated with pagan symbolism. We can’t confirm the apostles passed these traditions down, but we can confirm they don’t contradict the Gospel. These are, after all, essential facts of the Gospel.

Tradition can also serve as a guardrail to protect sound doctrine, like the Nicene Creed at the Council of Nicaea (325 AD). This was a prime example because the Creed drew on Scripture **and** apostolic tradition to defend Christ’s divinity. 

However, what began as a defense of truth also became the seed of compromise. Emperor Constantine, a man more politician than pastor, convened the Council.[^2] Though he favored Christianity, Constantine also sought imperial unity. Under his rule, bishops gained wealth and status while the Church grew entangled with political power. He also sought religious unity by decreeing a single day of rest, but on “the venerable day of the **Sun**,” not on the seventh day. During the Council of Nicaea, he quietly slipped in a rider, separating Passover from the biblical calendar, claiming the Church should have “nothing in common with the detestable Jews.”[^3] Those are the words of the “Christian” emperor Constantine. He set a precedent that future rulers of Rome would not just remember but codify.

Later emperors, such as Justinian, would enshrine those decisions in law, even declaring the bishop of Rome head of the universal Church.[^4] Here, the double edge of tradition became deadly. Traditions that were meant to reflect Scripture now began to replace it.

Basil of Caesarea (329–379 AD) argued that many practices rested on oral tradition rather than Scripture, such as making the sign of the cross, praying eastward, and observing Sunday as the Lord’s Day.[^5] But by Basil’s century, several traditions had already deviated from the faith once delivered (Jude 3). Though well-intentioned, this reasoning gave increasing weight to customs that were not just extra-biblical but also unbiblical.

This is why *sola Scriptura*, Scripture alone as the final authority, became so critical in the Reformation. When we trust the inspired Word, the Spirit does not scatter us; He unites us. Paul understood this principle so profoundly that he placed himself under the authority of the gospel he preached:

> “Even if we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel to you than what we have preached to you, let him be accursed.” (Galatians 1:8)

Think about that. Paul said if _he himself_ contradicted the gospel, the Galatians should reject him. He insisted that we test everything, even apostles, even angels, against the gospel already delivered.

A tradition that aligns with Scripture is a treasure, but one that conflicts with it becomes a trap. Jesus warned about this very thing:

> “You have made the commandment of God of no effect by your tradition... In vain they worship Me, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men.” (Matthew 15:6, 9)

In Jesus‘ time, several of the commandments were nullified by rabbinic traditions.[^6] But what traditions could the Church have developed that would have made “the commandment of God of no effect”? There are several cases, but the most boastful is the Sabbath.

### The Catholic Mark of Authority

Though Scripture confirms the seventh day was made holy by God, Catholic teaching openly claims the Church had the authority to change it. C.F. Thomas, chancellor to Cardinal Gibbons, wrote in 1895:

> “Of course, the Catholic Church claims that the change [from Sabbath to Sunday] was her act. And the act is a mark of her ecclesiastical power and authority in religious matters.”

The _Catholic Record_ echoed this in 1923:

> “Sunday is our mark of authority... The Church is above the Bible, and this transference of Sabbath observance is proof of that fact.”

And yet Yahweh declared in the 15th century BC:

> “You shall not add to the word that I command you, nor take from it.” (Deuteronomy 4:2)

If the Church claims power to alter God’s commandments, who is truly in charge? How can the Church assert authority over her King? Because of the power granted to the bishop of Rome to stand in the place of Christ on earth. The Pope is the Supreme Bishop, the literal king of the Vatican. But most blasphemously, he is called the Vicar of Christ.

### The Vicar of Christ

Daniel foresaw a ruler that would “speak pompous words against the Most High” and “intend to change times and law”.[^7] Paul called him “the man of lawlessness” who would exalt himself as if he were God.[^8]

The papal title _Vicar of Christ_ literally means “one who stands in Christ’s place.” Pope Pius X declared:

>“When one speaks of the Vicar of Christ, it is not necessary to examine but to obey.”

In recent times, Pope John Paul II affirmed:

>“The Pope is considered the man on earth who represents the Son of God, who ’takes the place‘ of the Second Person of the omnipotent God of the Trinity.”[^9]

The Reformers saw this position in the spirit that Daniel and Paul warned about. Hus, Luther, and Calvin all identified the papacy as the fulfillment of those prophecies. Keep in mind that it was not the person they sought to vilify, but the position. Hus warned that any person who took upon themselves such power should be called Antichrist.[^10] 

As Lord Acton wrote of popes and kings: “Power tends to corrupt, and absolute power corrupts absolutely.”[^11] This same pattern reappears wherever human charisma eclipses Christ’s kingship. And the modern Protestant Church is no exception.

### The Modern Mirror

This loss of divine authority is not just a Roman Catholic problem; it’s a universal one. The Protestant Church may not have emperors or popes at its center, but the pattern is strikingly similar. In many congregations, the life of the church still revolves around a single gifted communicator. Elders exist on paper. Ministries revolve around the stage. And the priesthood of all believers quietly yields to the sovereignty of one voice.

When we grow comfortable letting a pastor do our thinking, discernment gets outsourced. Scripture reading becomes sporadic. And in an environment where nobody tests the teachers, false doctrine and immorality flourish. Some modern teachers don’t just twist one or two commandments; they dismiss all ten as irrelevant. 

Scandals reveal the story of churches that have been built on personalities rather than the person of Christ. Worldly wealth has been hoarded. Abuse has been obscured from public scrutiny. Families have been shattered. Paul saw this coming:

> “From among yourselves men will rise up, speaking perverse things, to draw away the disciples after themselves.” (Acts 20:30)

It’s been happening in Rome for centuries. It’s still happening today in a church theater near you.

### Authority Restored

God’s authority, expressed through Scripture and the Spirit, has been forsaken in much of Christendom. Things have become so tragic among Church leadership that I’ve hesitated to quote anyone who hasn’t been dead for at least a decade. 

But there is hope. God has always preserved a remnant who guard His voice and test every claim by His Word—those like Wycliffe, Hus, and the Bereans. And we need them to rise again. 

What does that look like for us today?

First, it looks like serving, not lording. Jesus was clear: “The kings of the Gentiles exercise lordship over them... But not so among you” (Luke 22:25-26). Hierarchies create pyramids, and pyramids are ruled by pharaohs. The Church was never meant to be Egypt.

Second, it requires accountability through plurality and transparency. We need a plurality of leaders equipping a multitude of saints with a variety of gifts. But one Spirit. This is harder than simply trusting “the man of God.” It requires prayer, fasting, conversation, and consensus. It means no longer outsourcing truth, but learning to hear His still, small voice.

Third, it demands that we test everything, not with suspicion, but with love. We must collectively take on the posture of devoted disciples, by which we can test all things (1 Thessalonians 5:21).

These are demanding ingredients, but they’re the only recipe for unity. Wolves hunt the isolated. We need each other. We need to stand together behind the Shepherd, not cowering behind lesser men.

The question remains: **Who will we allow to command our worship—God or man?**

Our answer will shape everything, including what comes next. A Church without Christ as King will fill the gaps with grandeur over generosity, platforms over people, and budgets over the broken. In the next chapter, we’ll trace how misplaced authority led to misplaced treasure, and how we can chart a way back to what truly matters.

## Chapter 17: Lost Treasure

>We’ve built our towers, brick by brick,
With coffers deep and sermons slick.
But gold that gleams in vaulted halls,
Won’t feed the poor nor serve the small.

---

>“For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil...” (1 Timothy 6:10)

---

In the previous chapter, we examined how the Church lost biblical authority by replacing Christ’s headship with human hierarchy and elevating manmade traditions above His commandments. As the Church looked to man to replace the voice of the Spirit, they also began to substitute the treasure of heaven with the wealth of the world. What began with simplicity ballooned into a religious industry powered by professional clergy and costly cathedrals. Among the most twisted traditions that fueled this departure was the “tithe.”

Generosity has always been a godly principle, both in the Old and New Testaments. Biblical concepts of giving hold us to the highest standard of kindness toward the destitute. However, the modern doctrine of tithing was invented for another purpose: to feed the Beast.

### The Biblical Truth of Giving

Biblical tithing was far more complex than most realize. And it was focused on three different people groups through three different tithes. You heard that right. There wasn’t a tithe in the Bible. There were “tithes and offerings” as many preachers like to quote from Malachi 3:8. 

The first tithe went to the Levites, who had no inheritance (Numbers 18:24). They were not only the designated priests, but they were also not allowed to own land with which to make a living.

The second tithe was the festival tithe meant to be eaten in God’s presence during the Feasts of Yahweh (Deuteronomy 14:22-26). But if it was too burdensome to travel with your tithe (tithes were agricultural), then you could sell your tithe and bring the money to the Feasts. Then God commanded them to spend the money on “whatever your heart desires” and feast before the Lord (Deuteronomy 14:26)!

Every three years, Israel was to fill the storehouses with a third tithe. The storehouses were food pantries in Levite cities. And the goods did not go to a Temple building fund. They were meant for orphans, widows, traveling strangers, and the Levites (Deuteronomy 14:28-29).

And that was just the three tithes. One more surprise: many offerings were mandatory as well. That added up to about **23%** on average. And on top of all of that, God commanded them to provide generously for any brother falling on hard times (Deuteronomy 15:7–8).

The New Testament is relatively quiet on the law of tithes and offerings. They are referred to exactly once by Jesus, but in context, He is telling a Jew living under the Mosaic covenant that he should keep the law of Moses while not forsaking the moral Law of God.[^1] However, some argue that Jesus‘ affirmation of it proves that we should continue to give tithes according to the law of Moses. But here are some other points to consider:

1. We don’t believe circumcision is required. 
2. We don’t believe sacrifice is required.
3. And if we universally apply everything Jesus said, then why don’t we feel compelled to sell everything like He told the rich young ruler?
4. Still not convinced? Fine, how about that 23%?

I’ll admit this was VERY hard for me to wrap my head around when I first started studying the topic. I promise I won’t leave you hanging. I’ve written Appendix G to explore this further, but I’m not alone. Many other well-known and respected teachers of our time agree with me on this issue, including Francis Chan, John Piper, the late John MacArthur, and the late Voddie Baucham, among others.

Even though Old Testament tithing laws were obligatory, generosity was the main motivation. Israel was a theocracy, and these commandments were meant to demonstrate God’s loving kindness to the nations. The New Testament also promotes generosity, but of an even more radical kind. Yet, Paul could not have been clearer that nothing was compulsory:

> “So let each one give as he purposes in his heart, not grudgingly or of necessity; for God loves a cheerful giver.” (2 Corinthians 9:7)

Notice Paul says **not** to give “of necessity”. The word means pressure, force, or obligation. And yet, those words are painfully accurate when most people hear modern teaching about giving. 

### The Great Inversion

In Old Testament times, nearly all tithes and offerings went to the poor or Levites. A small portion went to the priests, a tithe of a tithe or 1%. And Temple upkeep? Just enough to maintain a place of worship.

Giving in the early Church looked radically different from what we see today. Their treasure was poured out for the broken and forgotten. Acts 4:34 says there was literally no poor among them who lacked. How crazy would it be to make that claim?

But today, most have inverted God’s design. If we look at where our time and money are invested today, it’s buildings first. Pastor salaries are second. And the poor get the leftovers.

When I first realized this, I was disgusted. But then I wondered if I was part of perpetuating the problem. It also made me ask: How did we get here?

### Where Did We Go Wrong?

I hate to pick on Constantine again, but after his contribution to Christianity, the Church began accumulating property, building elaborate structures, and supporting a professional clergy class that would have horrified the apostles. As the Church institution grew, so did its budget.

To fund this growing religious empire, a reliable revenue stream was needed.
It was found in a twisted interpretation of Mosaic law that demanded 10% be paid to the Church. At the Council of Macon (585), it was declared that those who refused to pay tithes were “robbers of God.” The penalty for neglecting the tithe was excommunication, which was considered a sentence to Hell.[^2] In 779, Charlemagne made tithing compulsory under civil law, adding civil punishments as the Church and the State merged into one.[^3]

Most agree that the breaking point came with indulgences. These were popularly regarded as “Get Out of Jail Free” coupons for the temporal punishment of sin in this life or the next. They were introduced in the 11th century during the Crusades and, by the 16th century, had degenerated into a kind of kingdom currency to fund the construction of St. Peter’s Basilica. The aggressive sale of these indulgences provoked Martin Luther’s 95 Theses, and ultimately sparked the Reformation.[^4]

But even after the Reformation, most Protestant churches kept the lucrative tradition of tithing alive, though they redirected the funds from Rome to their own institutions. In the United States, giving was initially voluntary, but as churches expanded their organizations, voluntary generosity gave way to mandatory tithing as the norm.[^5] By the early 20th century, mandatory tithing had become so entrenched in American Christianity that to question it was considered heresy. 

### The Truth That Sets Us Free

I want freedom for us all: no guilt, no treadmill of religious performance, just cheerful, Spirit-led generosity. I would rather see a joyful 1% than a terrified 10%. But only because I am convinced that when believers are freed from legalism, most will give _more_, not less.

Years ago, when I served as a campus pastor, my job performance was tied to attendance and giving. I asked an executive pastor, “If you’re going to measure me by what people give, let me teach the truth: not ‘10% or you’re cursed.’“ I told him, ”Those who already give 10% will keep doing so, but the thousand others who give nothing will start to give something.”

I told him about a single mom working three jobs who believed she was cursed if she couldn’t give 10%. I told her the biblical truth and encouraged her to start small, giving cheerfully whatever she could. Months later, she found me with bright eyes. She told me she had started small, like I told her. Then she leaned in and whispered with glee, “Today I gave 11%!” She laughed almost in disbelief, and we high-fived!

Now, that is a cheerful giver!

When I finished telling my story, the executive pastor slumped, and his eyes grew sad. He stuttered as he muttered, “I agree with you...But we can’t teach that.”

There it is. Not ignorance—incentive. The gatekeepers know the truth, but they also know the budget. This is not a new temptation. For centuries, Church authorities kept Scripture chained to pulpits and banned Bible reading. They feared that unlocking God’s Word would unseat their power. And they were right.

Francis Chan once said, “Money will change the way you do ministry.” Let that strike deep. The god of Mammon has made faithful servants of us who are willing to protect the system, at the cost of truth and freedom. We must return to simplicity, generosity, and accountability. 

That leaves us with an essential and practical question: if we refuse to feed the machine, _where should our treasure go?_

### Where Should Our Treasure Go?

If we’re freed from the law of tithing, what’s the call? The answer is radical, sacrificial, and joyful generosity.

The kind that:
- Cares for orphans and widows (James 1:27)
- Remembers the poor as a priority (Galatians 2:10)
- Ensures no one in the body lacks (Acts 4:34)
- Demonstrates hospitality to those who labor in the Word (1 Timothy 5:17-18)

Notice what’s **not** on that list? Multi-million-dollar buildings. Professional-grade production equipment. Pastor’s luxury, anything. 

I’m not saying buildings are evil or that excellent presentation is wrong. But when they consume the bulk of our resources while the world perishes, we’ve lost the meaning of the mission.

My wife and I live and give generously because our love for God compels us. We help people in crisis, stock food pantries, and support Christian schooling in war-torn Liberia. But we don’t just give money, we offer our time, our ear, our heart, and our home. Simple, relational hospitality is a lost art in our century.

**People over platforms.** That was the primary motivation for starting a simple house church. It’s also why I’ve been bi-vocational for almost 20 years rather than employed in full-time ministry. I’ve had a backstage pass to the worst of Church corruption. It can get ugly back there. Like Paul, I carry the weight of teaching the Gospel with such reverence that I refuse to allow my authority to ever hinder it (1 Corinthians 9:12, 18).

I tremble at the thought of standing empty-handed on Judgment Day,  because I left my treasures on earth and have nothing to show in heaven.

### Where is Our Treasure?

Jesus warned:

> “Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy and where thieves break in and steal; but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven... For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” (Matthew 6:19-21)

The church’s treasure reveals the church’s heart.

If our treasure is in buildings, that’s **where** our heart is.
If our treasure is in productions, that’s **where** our heart is.
If our treasure is in the poor, the broken, the lost, that’s **where** our heart is.

You see, we don’t get to say where our heart is. We can’t simply proclaim what’s most important. How we spend the time and resources God has given us tells Him and the world where our heart is. “For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.”

So, Church, where is our treasure?

Many churches allocate the vast majority of their resources to buildings, even if they are often used only once or twice a week. Buildings can facilitate mercy when they function as shelters, classrooms, or clinics. But we’ve constructed sensory palaces of worship with lights, fog machines, and concert-quality sound, while outside, people starve. 

If an outsider were hired to measure our treasure, who or what would they assume we worship?

I believe an outsider would look at the Church today and proclaim that we worship the Temple more than the one who fills the Temple.

The early Church turned the world upside down with just Jesus and radical generosity that made them wonder, **“Who are these people?”** Like the lost foundation of authority, I hope you agree that misplacing our treasure is serious and tragic. But did you know that this was also prophesied? Revelation states that the Apostate Church will become so wealthy that the merchants of the earth will mourn as she is destroyed because of how much the Church enriched them through her “abundance and luxury.” She is depicted as wearing fine linen “adorned with gold and precious stones and pearls” (Revelation 18:3, 16).

I’ll borrow a provocative line from the founder of _The Religion Business_, Nathan Apffel. He said that the Church is a Body. And do you know what we call a body that sells itself? A prostitute. A harlot. And who enjoys a prostitute? People who want to satisfy their own fleshly desires.[^6] They are called adulterers.

My friends, that is not the Bride of Christ. That is the Whore of Babylon. 

If this stings, I’m not calling you an adulterer; I’m warning us all how easy the heart can wander. The path to restore our lost treasure will be a bold one. Some pastors may need to become bi-vocational. Some church buildings may need to be sold or repurposed. However, we should all start demanding accountability and transparency. That’s the only way to eliminate the disease and ensure it never returns.

But, again I say, Church:

>“Come out of her, my people, lest you share in her sins, and lest you receive of her plagues.” (Revelation 18:4)

In the next chapter, we’ll see how this trail of lost authority and lost treasure also led us to forsake our first love.

## Chapter 18: Lost Love

>Truth alone is cold and bare,  
Lest it lead the heart somewhere.  
Rushing wind, delight, desire,
Fans a coal into a fire.

---

>”And because lawlessness will abound, the love of many will grow cold.” (Matthew 24:12)

---

>God designed the human machine to run on Himself. He Himself is the fuel our spirits were designed to burn. (C.S. Lewis, Mere Christianity, paraphrase)

---

I didn’t mean to write a book like this. At first, I wanted it to be academic: clean, clear, defined—a theological answer to a spiritual crisis. But as I churned out chapters, a deeper ache arose. The script flipped. I started to wrestle with questions that bothered me.

Not just what is true, but why does it matter? Not just what happened in church history, but what happens if we don’t remember what we’ve lost?

As I pondered those questions, a great fear grew in my heart: teaching facts without fueling faith. After all, doctrine is dead if it doesn’t lead to a living Christ. As Paul wrote, knowledge puffs up, but love builds up.[^1] As A.W. Tozer once wrote:

>For it is not mere words that nourish the soul, but God Himself, and unless and until the hearers find God in personal experience they are not the better for having heard the truth.“[^2]

Once Constantine opened the arms of Rome to the Church, acceptance opened the door to cultural Christianity. Crudely, persecution filtered out the weak of faith. However, as the Church accumulated power and privilege, love for worldly things gradually replaced love of the divine. Formality replaced informality, rows replaced circles, and the priesthood of an elite supplanted the priesthood of all. And at the pinnacle of corruption, manmade tradition fully usurped the commandments of God. That lawlessness bred aloofness, and as Jesus predicted, the love of many grew cold.

However, even before Constantine, other churches in history had been guilty of the same offense. Jesus praised the church in Ephesus in Revelation 2:2-6. He commended them for being hardworking, exposing false apostles and rejecting false teachers who led others astray. And yet, Jesus also warned them. He said they had lost their ”first love.“ He begged them to repent and return to the simplicity of loving Him and loving each other. They had the sound doctrine and were zealous for the truth, but their hearts had still grown cold. That should be a caution for us all.

So, how do we keep our hearts burning?

### The Secret Place

There’s a significant difference between the crowd and those who draw near. Jesus drew this distinction:

> “But when He was alone, those around Him with the twelve asked Him about the parable. And He said to them, ‘To you it has been given to know the mystery of the kingdom of God; but to those who are outside, all things come in parables.’” (Mark 4:10–11)

Jesus didn’t share His heart with casual listeners. He reserved His mysteries for those who lingered after the crowds dispersed. Intimacy with Him wasn’t found in public spectacle; it was found in private devotion. That’s the Secret Place, where authentic discipleship is cultivated.[^3]

> “But you, when you pray, go into your room, and when you have shut your door, pray to your Father who is in the secret place; and your Father who sees in secret will reward you openly.” (Matthew 6:6)

The crowd chases bread and fish. The masses gather for miracles. But disciples rise early to meet Him in the quiet morning hours, long before the big event. Disciples hang around after, patiently waiting for the Master to be alone again. Disciples long for the Secret Place.

### The Lost Art of Meditation

We obviously can’t follow Jesus around everywhere like the first disciples. Today, we must immerse ourselves in holy stillness to find Him; some call that meditation. It may be that we lost our first love because we also lost the art of meditation. It is one of the most countercultural things we can do, and it is necessary if we desire to know God.

J.I. Packer, author of Knowing God, once said of meditation:

>”Christian people suffer grievously from their ignorance of the practice.“[^4]

I could not agree more with Dr. Packer. I’ve seen firsthand in my life and in others a direct correlation between spiritual health and the quality of one’s prayerful and meditative practice. The problem is that we’ve made intimacy with God optional, as if deep friendship with Him belongs to the super spiritual.

But let’s say it plainly: If you don’t spend regular, unrushed, quality time with God, you don’t know Him. You may have experienced His grace, perhaps even have saving faith, but remain far from the closeness He desires. God is often relegated to being a non-urgent, non-important item on our to-do list. We might be faithful to attend a church service once a week, but private time with Him is not found on our calendar. And yet, until everyday things struggle to find space in our schedules because we’re so captivated by Him, we haven’t fully tasted the passion of the Cross.

### Cultural Christianity

In the Church today, so many want to want Him, but they are ultimately unmoved. It reminds me of a plant-buying spree I went on a while back. I was suffering from severe sleep apnea at the time, and the idea of more oxygen in my office sounded very appealing. I could have bought a couple of small plants to adorn my desk, but instead I purchased nine—all different kinds. 

I loved my little mini jungle for about two weeks, until it was time to figure out what these plants needed to survive. I started reading with enthusiasm, but that passion quickly gave way to a sense of disdain. My lack of motivation reminded me of my lack of a green thumb. I wanted the plants to thrive, but I didn’t have the will to water them, even every other week.

Like my plants, our love for Jesus has withered, not from lack of interest, but from lack of intention. This parallels the cultural Christianity of our time. Most of us want to know Jesus; we want to experience the power and presence of the One True God. But we don’t want to do anything to meet Him. And I know why.

It’s because we aren’t desperate for Him. He’s one thing among many, not our all. He’s our weekend side hustle, while we refuse to be His faithful bride. Christianity in so many circles has become a buffet, not for the hungry, but for the picky. And beneath that mixed devotion lurks a crushing realization: We want to want Him, but we don’t want to pay the price.

And so He waits. 

And as A.W. Tozer once wrote, “He waits to be wanted. Too bad that with many of us, He waits so long, so very long, in vain.”[^5]

The solution to our spiritual apathy is what I call **desperate delight**: A hunger both holy and hopeful.

### Desperate Delight

Surrender is not where the struggle begins; it starts with desire. The deeper issue isn’t our will but our wanting. As I’ve heard it said humorously, our ”wanter“ is broken.

So how do we fix it? It begins with desperation. Desperation is not just emotional intensity. It’s the soul’s admission: **There’s nowhere else to go.**

The word desperate comes from the Latin *desperatus*, meaning “to lose hope.” Hope, in the modern English sense, has come to mean wishful thinking. But in the biblical sense, hope is not vague optimism. It is the confident expectation of good. So to be desperate for God is to have lost hope that good can come from anywhere else.

That’s holy desperation. That is desperate delight: the realization that God alone is good.

This is where it all went wrong in the Garden of Eden. Adam and Eve had only known goodness as coming from God. But the Serpent planted a seed of doubt. Suddenly, Adam and Eve looked at the forbidden fruit and thought, “Maybe this is good too,” thus sin entered into humanity. Sin begins when we believe there’s any good apart from God. Sin is the opposing perspective to holy desperation. 

### Send Me

When Isaiah saw God in His glory, he cried out:

>“Woe to me! I am undone!” (Isaiah 6:5)

The word undone means to unravel, to cease to exist. In God’s holy light, Isaiah saw God’s pure goodness, but he also saw his own depravity, and the gap was too much to bear. Isaiah mourned:

>“I am a man of unclean lips… among a people of unclean lips.” 

Then a seraph touches his lips with a coal from the altar: 

>“Your sin is atoned for.”

And immediately the voice of Yahweh thundered:

>“Whom shall I send?”

Without hesitation, the newly cleansed Isaiah shouted:

>“Here I am! Send me.”[^6]

That is total surrender beget from holy desperation. A vision of God’s goodness so blinding that it exposed every other pursuit as false, lacking any good compared to Yahweh. That kind of desperation doesn’t come from willpower. It comes from perspective, being fully convinced that He alone is good. The want of Isaiah’s heart was purified in the presence of the Almighty.

Holy desperation changes everything. Fir it is His goodness that leads us not only to repentance, but to intimacy (Romans 2:4). We no longer read Scripture to finish a chapter; we read to meet Someone. We don’t pray because we should, we pray because we miss Him.

The Psalmist says, “Delight yourself also in the Lord, and He will give you the desires of your heart”.[^7] The Hebrew word for “delight” means to be soft, pliable.[^8] It’s not about getting what we want; it’s about becoming moldable in His hands. As our hearts soften, He shapes our desires to reflect His own. And when we’re desperate for Him, we finally say, not just with words but with every fiber of our being: “Here I am! Send me.”

That same call of God is extended to all of us. And each of us must answer in turn, or else remain silent until He passes us by.

### Personal Surrender

When I surrendered my life to Jesus, I wasn’t at rock bottom in the way most people think. I was a star football player and a straight-A student. I was in with the popular crowd. I partied hard, got drunk every Friday night, and lived like I had everything I wanted, everything the world promised would make me happy, and yet I was hollow.

One night, I woke up with alcohol poisoning, but for the first time, a sober clarity. The only thing I hadn’t tried was giving God the chance to be God. So I did. I turned to Him in desperation, not casually, but earnestly. And He met me. He didn’t just patch me up. He filled the void. He satisfied my thirsty soul. I completely believed that if He wasn’t good, then nothing was good. 

And we need that perspective, not just once, but every day. Surrender isn’t just letting go of old vices. It’s saying yes, and saying yes again. Yes to His call. Yes to the cost. Yes to Him.

But we will only say ”yes“ if we see Him as the only good worth pursuing. In seeing Him, we know Him. In knowing Him, we see Him. Thus, our passion fuels our passion like a holy chain reaction—nuclear with the fire of God. 

That is love, first love, made new for Jesus. Created in desperation and cultivated in the Secret Place. *Have you met Him there today?*

## Chapter 19: Lost Power

>The narrow way the prophets said,
Runs red with fire and blood that’s shed.
Not wide, not safe, not praised by men—

>We’ve traded flame for form and name,

---
>“But know this, that in the last days perilous times will come: For men will be lovers of themselves, lovers of money...lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God, having a form of godliness but denying its power.” (2 Timothy 3:1–2, 4–5)
---

>“I am not afraid that the people called Methodists should ever cease to exist... but I am afraid lest they should only exist as a dead sect, having the form of religion without the power.” - John Wesley [^1]

There is a mystery tucked into the Gospels, one that’s been debated, even removed in some modern translations. It’s a secret hidden in plain sight, a practice so foundational yet so neglected in our time.

Fasting.

With the lost foundation of authority, we lost the careful study of the Word. When we lost our treasure, we forsook generous and cheerful giving. Our love cooled with the lost art of meditation, and in like fashion, it seems, our power has perished along with the fading practice of fasting. 

Fasting is the forgotten fragrance of holy desperation.

The Gospels record an incident in which the disciples failed to cast out a demon. In the story, it doesn’t say that Jesus blamed the boy. He didn’t even blame the demon. Jesus turned to His own disciples and said:

> “This kind can come out by nothing but prayer and fasting” (Mark 9:29)

Those words, “and fasting,” are absent from most modern translations. You’ll find them footnoted, bracketed, or removed entirely. But why? That’s a serious question. And we’d do well to ask it.[^2]

Who profits when the key to deliverance is footnoted? You can feel the tension in that omission. If fasting truly is essential to driving out darkness, if it unlocks spiritual clarity, and if it is a weapon in the war against hell, then you’d do well to believe that the Enemy would want those words forgotten.

### The Living Sacrifice

Fasting isn’t a diet; it’s a doorway to divine power and a means of hearing God. It’s an offering, a sacred act of surrender, where your heart is the altar, but your very body becomes the sacrifice.

Leviticus 3:16 says, “The fat belongs to the Lord.” It was the fat of the sacrifice, the richest part, that was a “sweet aroma” before God. Romans 12:1 reminds us that we are called to be a living sacrifice. When we fast, our bodies begin to burn their own fat, and, in a very real, physical sense, we become a living sacrifice, holy and pleasing to God. It’s a smoke signal before the Almighty that we yearn for something eternal, more than food or comfort. And that kind of worship moves heaven and earth.

This isn’t just theory or theology. I’ve seen this firsthand, in one of the most painful and miraculous seasons of my life and that of my wife.

### When Fasting Led to Healing

Years ago, my wife was diagnosed, out of nowhere, with Hepatitis C. It blindsided us. She had no risk factors, no explanation from the doctors, nothing. It was discovered only because she’d gone to donate blood. And suddenly she was thrust into a world of viral loads, grim prognoses, and pressure to begin treatment immediately.

At the time, the standard therapy was brutal with harsh side effects and a low success rate. But as we prayed, my wife felt strongly that the Lord was calling her to wait. Not to refuse medicine, but to seek Him first. And so, she did. She waited. And waited. And waited.

For two years.

The doctors grew frustrated, then angry. Her viral load was in the millions, and they warned her she was gambling with her life. And still, she waited not out of fear, but out of faith. She wanted to hear clearly from God, but all she could sense was the Spirit begging pause.

And then one day, she decided to fast, not for healing, but for understanding. After several days of fasting, the Lord impressed something specific on her heart: Talk to this doctor. Not just any doctor, a name she’d never thought to seek out before. There was no reason to think this doctor would be any different than all the others, but we scheduled the appointment. 

Unsurprisingly, the new doctor said the same thing as the others. He told us this was serious and she needed to begin treatment now. Our guts sank. It felt like we had waited in vain.

But then something unusual happened. The doctor paused, glanced down at his watch, and his entire demeanor shifted. He began asking rapid-fire questions, some of them oddly specific. “Have you eaten anything today?” he asked.

Normally, she would have. It was already afternoon. But that day, because she was fasting, she answered, “No.”

He nodded quickly. “Good. Then we can draw labs.” Just then, his phone rang. He stepped out, listened for a moment, then came back in with urgency in his voice.

“There’s an experimental trial across town. It’s your best chance. But you don’t have time to think about it here. The application closes in thirty minutes, and the clinic is thirty minutes away. Go now, you’ll have to decide on the drive.”

We looked at each other. *Was this it? Did God want her to be a lab rat?!*

We ran to the car, and on the drive over, my wife remembered what the Lord had spoken to her that morning: 

>“The steps of a good man are ordered by the LORD, and He delights in his way.” (Psalm 37:23)

We felt it in our bones. As crazy as it seemed, this was the moment we had waited for. And only because she had never taken the previous treatment, she was eligible for this study.

Long story short, that trial became the cure for Hepatitis C. Today, she is fully healed. Not only that, but she was actually paid to participate in a study for a medicine that would have cost over $100,000.

We didn’t reject medicine. We trusted that God was leading even if we couldn’t hear clearly at first. But that miracle never would have happened if she hadn’t fasted. Fasting didn’t change God. It changed her. And it opened her ears to hear so that she might be healed.

### When Healing Led to Hearing

I’ve also seen this in my own body.

A few months after my wife was healed, she began pressing me to fast as well. I had been battling severe degenerative disc disease in my lower back for almost a decade. I mentioned this at the beginning of the book. I was on the verge of disability. Painkillers barely dulled the edge. My only hope was to fast. I believed God had spoken a promise to me: that if I fasted, He would heal my back. And then He whispered something more: that He would give me a revelation of Revelation.

Eventually, I fasted, reluctantly, but obediently. But after three days, I wasn’t better. I was worse. I was in agony. Lying there on my couch, I literally sobbed. I begged God. I asked what I had done wrong. I wondered if I misunderstood His promise. But in the middle of that suffering, I saw something strange: A flash in my spirit of a demon stabbing me in the back.

I didn’t know if it was the Lord or just the pain. But I decided that if it was God showing me how to pray, then that’s how I’d pray. And so I prayed:

> “God, set me free from this demonic attack on my back. In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth. Amen.”

And I kid you not: in that moment, when I said “Amen,” all the pain left. All of it. I collapsed in exhaustion and woke up hours later, completely healed. I’ve been healed ever since.

But there was more. 

The second part of the promise was this: that if I fasted, not only would I be healed, but I would hear. In the days that followed, I continued to fast, and He answered. The words He spoke then became the seed of this very book.

This message was born in that fire, from years of suffering that led to surrender. Since then, I’ve heard God with clarity, but also with trepidation. It’s a heavy gift to carry. But I would never trade this intimacy for superficiality.

### The Forgotten Baptism

Scripture is full of this pattern: fasting, surrender, and calling. Before Moses received the Law, he fasted for forty days. Before Elijah heard the still, small voice, he fasted. Before Jesus launched His ministry, He was led into the wilderness, where He fasted. Before Paul was sent out, the Church fasted and laid hands on him.

And yet we treat fasting like an optional upgrade in our walk with God. But the prophets didn’t. The apostles didn’t. And neither did Jesus. In the early Church, it was customary to fast before water baptism and then twice a week thereafter.[^3] Jesus also said, “When you fast…”, not if.[^4] And He promised that when we fast in the Secret Place, the Father who sees in secret will reward us.

Sometimes that reward is the power to heal the sick. Sometimes it’s perspective. Sometimes it’s prophetic clarity. Sometimes it’s just Him. But He always rewards. 

Do you **want** that reward?

Honestly, most people aren’t just afraid of fasting; they are scared of the power that comes with it. I don’t know many people who want the responsibility of prophecy, or any other gift. And speaking of baptism by water, what about baptism in the Spirit?

Do you think this is too high a standard for the average Christian? Do you think this power is reserved for a spiritual elite?

Hear Peter’s words about the qualifications of those chosen to wait tables:  
They must be “full of the Holy Spirit and wisdom.”[^5]

Hear Paul urge every believer:  

>“**Eagerly** desire the gift of prophecy.” (1 Corinthians 14:1, emphasis added)

Hear Moses when Joshua feared Moses‘ leadership would be threatened by others being filled with the Spirit:

> “Oh, that all the Lord’s people were prophets, and that the Lord would put His Spirit upon them!” (Numbers 11:29)

And his longing wasn’t just a wish; it became a prophecy.  Yahweh declared through Joel:

>“And it shall come to pass in the last days that I will pour out My Spirit on all flesh; your sons and your daughters shall prophesy…” (Joel 2:28–29)

This isn’t just for the anointed or ordained. It’s for **all**. And without it, we risk becoming something far less than the Church Christ intended.

Paul met men who had been baptized by John the Baptist, the greatest prophet according to Jesus, and still Paul insisted that wasn’t enough. He laid his hands on them that they might also receive the baptism of the Holy Spirit.[^6]

We need that baptism too.

### A Test of Faith

Almost a year after my healing, I found myself facing an even greater test of faith, not just believing for my own healing, but for that of someone else.

We were hosting a week of worship and prayer. As had become my habit, I was fasting, wanting to be ready for whatever God might want to do. On the final night of the worship week, a call was given for anyone who wanted one last opportunity for prayer to come forward. Several hundred people flocked to the edge of the stage. Pastors and leaders moved through the crowd, praying with as many as they could. 

As people were being prayed for, I looked up and saw a woman with her hands raised, and then I saw something else. In my spirit, I saw my hands reaching in through her back, pulling out a cancerous mass from her lungs, dark and disgusting. As I saw this, I heard a voice within say, “Lymph nodes… cervix.”

After about fifteen minutes, the crowd subsided. The emcee thanked everyone for attending, then offered a prayer and dismissed us.
As the room began to clear, the woman turned and started walking away.

I hesitated. I was nervous. But I believed, _really_ believed, I was supposed to pray for her. The words of Mark 16:17-18 echoed in my heart:

> “And these signs will follow those who believe… they will lay hands on the sick, and they will recover.”

I ran over to her. I introduced myself, bracing for a painfully awkward moment. _What do I even say?_ I wondered.

Then I just blurted out, “You have cancer. Is that right?”

She froze, mouth wide open, and then began to weep. Through tears, she managed a broken, “Yes!”

I gently asked, “Is it in your cervix and lymph nodes?”

She nodded, still crying. I asked where else, and she told me her lungs and kidneys. She had been diagnosed with small-cell carcinoma. The doctors had given her two weeks to live—**exactly** two weeks prior.

That was all the confirmation I needed. My wife and I laid hands on her, and I prayed boldly, declaring she was healed in the name of Jesus Christ. She cried, hugged us as her family stood nearby, stunned. Then she left, and I never heard anything again, not until six months later.

I happened to be speaking with a friend who knew her. I was told that she made a full recovery after that night! Her cancer had gone into complete remission, her life extended by the power of God. But I wonder: how many more are waiting for us to believe boldly? 

What faith lies dormant because we hear not?

What miracles have passed by the broken and destitute because we fast not?

Love is greater than all the gifts, but Paul still pleaded with the Church:

> “Pursue love, and desire spiritual gifts, but especially that you may prophesy.” (1 Corinthians 14:1)

Will we be among those in the last days who possess a form of godliness, but no real power to set others free?

Or will we be counted among those who were baptized not only in water, but in Spirit and fire?

Friends, if you’ve ever asked where all the prophets have gone, maybe they haven’t disappeared. Perhaps they’ve just been feasting when they should have been fasting.

## Chapter 20: Lost Mission

> The Ark is not a building.  
> The Church is not a brand.  
> The Body is not a business.  
> That Kingdom—will not—stand.

I want to circle back to the day God healed my spine, to the promised revelation that followed. It began that very evening, when God invited me on a late-night walk. I have never heard Him so clearly, before or since. Under streetlights and stars, He unfolded a vision; the Architect sketching His house on my heart. Only a few words could capture it: **the Church without walls.**

Back then, I didn’t fully grasp it after ten years in a thriving megachurch where hundreds of baptisms dazzled, and worship echoed like a stadium. And yet, I knew something was missing, because lasting transformation never stuck. And for every new face walking in, another quietly slipped away. We called it the **back-door problem**.

But now I understand: we weren’t just missing a strategy. We were missing the seven foundations. We’ve lost much over the centuries. We’ve lost our memory of His holy days. We’ve lost His Sabbath, His authority, and the hope of treasure in heaven. We’ve lost our first love and the power of His Spirit. And in losing all of this, we’ve lost the mission itself.

The mission is to make disciples who will stand on the Day of Judgment. But in forsaking the mission, we’ve traded making disciples for building empires. We are called to be master builders, but not of buildings—of people.[^1]

>“For we are God’s fellow workers; you are God’s field, you are God’s building. According to the grace of God which was given to me, as a wise master builder I have laid the foundation, and another builds on it. But let each one **take heed** how he builds on it.” (1 Corinthians 3:9–10, emphasis added)

“Take heed,” Paul says. Models aren’t sacred; the mission is. But forms can change, must change, if they hinder our purpose.

### The Ark

There was a time when God called Nehemiah to rebuild Jerusalem’s walls.[^2] Back then, there was one Temple, one Ark, and one mission: rebuild what man had torn down. But today, the Spirit begs us to build something different. Today, we are called to construct an Ark of His presence spread across the globe, forged for what is ahead. What’s coming is imminent, like the flood of Noah. And, as with the call given to Noah, we’ve been asked to prepare an Ark.

I had a vision of such an Ark, but it sounds strange at first. I saw hundreds of pink packing peanuts crammed into tiny clear cylinders. Then an angel pressed the cylinders together to form Noah’s Ark. 

Packing peanuts are a simple invention. Without pressure, they are scattered and useless. But when pressure is applied, they link and lock together, forming a solid structure. They protect what matters most.

The pink packing peanuts symbolize us. The cylinders represent believers gathered in tight circles rather than rows. They are the Church without walls. An Ark of God. Ready for the flood. But this vision wasn’t just about connection, it was about preparation. The Ark wasn’t decorative; it was life-saving.

In the West, our comfort may subtly shape our End-time expectations, supposing that God will spare us from tribulation before His return. But for many believers in places like Nigeria, China, and Iraq, where extreme trial is their daily reality. Regardless of your eschatology, Jesus clearly warns us that we will have tribulation in this life.[^3] So then, are we ready for that?

Noah built the Ark before it rained, not after. That’s not a minor detail. That’s what faith looks like. But friend, if Noah waited for the rain before he swung the hammer, the Ark would’ve been a coffin, not a lifeboat.

Even for those who believe tribulation is coming, there seems to be a quiet assumption that we’ll have ample warning. But COVID-19 in 2020 taught us how fast everything can shift. One day, churches were full; the next, doors were locked by decree. 

What happens when tax exemptions vanish, or preaching the Bible is labeled hate speech? When bank accounts are frozen, and buildings are seized? These aren’t dystopian fantasies; they’re already unfolding.[^8] And if your church isn’t prepared for this now, it’s not ready for the flood.

### The Last Call to Come Out

The Scriptures present a sobering view of what occurs when a people refuse to repent and choose to remain within a system destined for destruction. Jericho fell at the blast of the trumpet. And one day, the kingdoms of this world will melt like wax at the sound of the last trumpet. Revelation echoes the story of Jericho: seven trumpets that precede judgment.[^4] Even the march around Jericho foretells the final judgment. For seven days, seven priests circled, blowing seven trumpets to warn all that dwelt within.

Recall Rahab, the harlot of Jericho. What marked Rahab then marks the Remnant Church today. But the contrast with the Whore of Babylon is stunning. Unlike Rahab, Babylon does not repent. She does not choose Yahweh. She is adorned with religion but filled with rebellion. God’s last call is not to Babylon, but to His people caught in her midst:

>“Come out of her, My people, lest you share in her sins, lest you receive of her plagues.” (Revelation 18:4)

But what does it mean to “come out”? Rahab didn’t escape Jericho before the walls fell. Her house was built into the wall itself, yet she was preserved *through* judgment while the city crumbled around her, just as Israel wasn’t evacuated from Egypt before the plagues, but set apart in Goshen.

To “come out” is not necessarily a call to leave a building or an institution; it’s a call to be holy or set apart. If you are a part of a church willing to reclaim these lost foundations, bloom where you’re planted. But if the walls around you refuse to be changed by truth, don’t cling to the rubble. The call remains:

Trust the scarlet mark.
Join the people of God.

This isn’t a distant warning; it’s here. The spirit of apostasy has long crept into churches, hiding behind crosses and cathedrals, where eternal truth is bartered for human tradition. I beg you, dear friend, see it for what it is. Choosing between the Two Churches is as crucial as choosing between the Two Marks. Each Church bears a Mark. Allegiance must be pledged.

But here is the good news: anyone who heeds His call is welcomed and woven into His Remnant. Because He’s not just calling us out of something; He’s calling us into something.

### The Church Without Walls

The Ark we’re called to build isn’t a luxury yacht; it’s a refuge. Not made of wood and pitch but of righteousness and relationship. Not a place, but a people; a Church without walls. Jesus didn’t say, “Go build churches.” He said clearly: “Go and make disciples.”[^5] But what does that look like? 

It’s not about inviting people to a service; it’s about inviting them into your life. Paul captured this well:

> “So, affectionately longing for you, we were well pleased to impart to you not only the gospel of God, **but also our own lives**...” (1 Thessalonians 2:8, emphasis added)

This kind of discipleship doesn’t take place from a pulpit; it happens around a kitchen table, in the grit of everyday life. Because discipleship, the Jesus way, is costly. We still need preaching and teaching. But the sweat of a thousand sermons does not compare to the weight of one tear shed while holding a hand in despair.

Hear again, “take heed how you build,” Paul implores as he also warns:

> “Now if anyone builds on this foundation with gold, silver, precious stones, wood, hay, straw, each one’s work will become clear; for the Day will declare it, because it will be revealed by fire; and the fire will test each one’s work, of what sort it is. If anyone’s work which he has built on it endures, he will receive a reward. If anyone’s work is burned, he will suffer loss; but he himself will be saved, yet so as through fire.” (1 Corinthians 3:12–15)

The “work” he speaks of is the quality and cost of discipleship. A sober thought. But here is a sobering question: 

Will our work stand the impending test of fire?

### Where Do We Go From Here?

My response to all this has been a struggle, as I expect it will be for others. How do we leave something so comfortable, familiar, and “successful” for something we can barely explain?

When I first set out on this path, I thought I was alone. I felt a bit like Elijah, when he thought he was by himself to stand against the sea of apostasy in Israel. But God told Elijah there were seven thousand others who had not bowed the knee.[^6] So, I also found a trail already blazed, even if it was faint and far off the well-trodden way.

For me, this journey meant leaving the Bible Belt and starting over in a new place: Denver, Colorado. Before we left, a pastor friend warned me, “Denver is where church plants go to die.” That’s when it hit me. We weren’t moving to Denver to plant churches. We were moving to plant ourselves—and **simply** make disciples.

Almost everything we do is simple, but let me tell you, it is not easy. In the early days, we prayed often, fasted often, and shared our vision with anyone who would listen. Eventually, a few families gathered who knew they needed “more.” Not more flash or formality. More of Jesus. More depth. More focus and intention on knowing and being known. And what began plainly had to remain plain. 

I once heard Francis Chan say, “What you draw people with is what you have to keep them with.” We didn’t want to draw people with anything but Jesus. So we went back to the early Church. Whatever they were devoted to, we would focus on. According to Acts 2, they devoted themselves to studying the Word, to fellowship, to breaking bread, and to prayer.[^7] They met in each other’s homes and in the temple. They were generous toward one another and enjoyed a simple but sincere love for all.

So that’s what we did.

Everything became about fostering and cultivating those fundamental qualities. But it required unlearning. We still carried a particular lens, a way of seeing the world, the Church, God, and the Scriptures, shaped by modern church culture.

Take one small example. What do you imagine when you read that the early Church met daily in the temple? Do you imagine Peter or James standing behind a pulpit, preaching the risen Christ while Pharisees sit in theater-style seats, arms crossed, thinking, _“Why did we give Peter the microphone?”_ Do you picture Jerusalem’s finest choir as the backdrop while Mary Magdalene jams on the harp?

Meeting in the temple was nothing like the megachurch coliseums of today. The disciples weren’t headliners; they were persecuted for following the so-called heretic, Jesus.

Here’s another example. When we first started gathering, we thought one of our most significant problems would be how to simulcast my teaching from my living room. I cringe now, and laugh, so I don’t cry, remembering how much that idea missed the point. But we grew. We learned. And God graciously guided us by His Spirit.

Much more has been written by others about what it can look like to lead what some call an “organic” or “simple” church. I’ve included a brief Appendix H for the brave at heart, along with several resources to help those who want to make the jump or cautiously test the waters.

But let me end with one last challenge.

One of my passions is Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu. I started training in the late 90s, and after many years, I finally earned my black belt. I suspect that most people want to train up before they show up because they are afraid. They fear getting hurt or just having their pride wounded. And I’ll admit, Jiu-Jitsu can be both humbling and dangerous. But so it is with you, dear Christian. If you want to learn the lost art of Christianity, you must—wait for it—begin.

Start small. Start where you are. Invite someone to your table. Fast and pray. Open the Scriptures with your family. Rest on His Sabbath. Listen for His voice. Whatever you do, show up, and keep showing up. **There just isn’t another formula.**

Remember, you are not alone. The same God who preserved a remnant in Elijah’s day is preserving one now. He led my family and me from comfort into the unknown. He can lead you too, step by step, surrender after surrender, until the Church without walls is no longer just a vision, but the lived reality of the next generation, and perhaps the last.

## Appendix A: The Passing of Passover

> A table set, a Lamb once slain,
> The dawn of peace, yet washed in pain.
> His call remains, though many strayed,
> Remember Me—this holy day.

---

>“As often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death till He comes.” (1 Corinthians 11:26)

---

Under the shadow of Rome’s hatred and the Church’s growing fear of association with Judaism, one of the first sacred threads to unravel was Passover—the very feast Christ commanded us to keep in remembrance of Him.

Imagine sitting at that table. The air heavy with the scent of roasted lamb, the flicker of oil lamps casting shadows on His eager but serious stare. Jesus lifts the bread, breaks it, and says, “This is My body.” He raises the cup, His eyes piercing each soul, and declares, “This is My blood.” It wasn’t just a meal—it was a moment that redefined everything.

For over a thousand years, Passover had been Israel’s heartbeat. A night of remembrance, marking the day God’s hand swept through Egypt, sparing those under the blood of the lamb. Every bite of unleavened bread, every sip of wine, told the story of deliverance. Then Jesus took that ancient feast and expanded its meaning. “Do this,” He said, “in remembrance of Me.”

For the first followers of Jesus, this was sacred. This wasn’t optional. It wasn’t a suggestion. It was a command tied to the rhythm of God’s calendar. On the fourteenth day of the first month—God’s appointed time—they remembered. They cleansed their homes of leaven, a sign of being set apart.  
They remembered not just freedom from Pharaoh, but freedom from sin and death itself. For over a hundred years, this practice remained unchanged in the Church.

Paul reminded the early Church of this sacred pattern when he wrote: “For indeed Christ, our Passover, was sacrificed for us. Therefore let us keep the feast… with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth” (1 Corinthians 5:7–8).

But something happened. Slowly, subtly, the meal faded. What was once sacred became scattered. What was once unified became divided. How did we lose it?

It didn’t happen overnight. After the apostles died, the Church grew—beautifully at first. Then Rome rose, Jerusalem fell, and the faith drifted with every passing year.

The passing of Passover is often called the Quartodeciman Controversy—a fancy name for a fight over dates. But this was no petty argument. It was a battle over who defines the faith: God or man.

After the Jewish revolts, suspicion and hostility toward anything “Jewish” intensified. And slowly—Passover was passed away.

A new calendar emerged.  A new tradition took its place.

Not because God commanded it.  
Not because the apostles taught it.  
But because the Roman bishop decided it so.

### Polycarp vs Anicetus

Polycarp had walked with John the Apostle. He learned from the man who leaned on Jesus’ chest and was considered “the one whom Jesus loved.” Polycarp knew the teachings of Christ intimately.

And he refused to compromise.

In 155 AD, Anicetus, Bishop of Rome, declared that Passover should no longer be kept on God’s appointed day but moved to a Sunday. At the age of eighty-six, Polycarp traveled to Rome to confront him.

The meeting was tense. Neither man yielded.

Polycarp insisted he followed the example of Jesus and the apostles [^38.1]. But Anicetus asserted his own authority. Their debate ended in a deadlock, and they parted ways.

But something strangely tragic happened next.

Once Polycarp arrived back home, he was captured by Roman authorities. He was given a chance to pay homage to Caesar and renounce Christ.

Again, he refused to compromise.

His reply is now legendary:

> “Eighty and six years I have served Him, and He has done me no wrong. How then can I blaspheme my King and Savior?” [^38.2]

They burned him alive. The flames rose, and when they failed to consume him, a spear pierced his side—on the very day of Passover [^38.3]. Coincidence? Or a final testimony to the truth he died for? 

### The Power Play

A generation later, the conflict over Passover came to a breaking point.

Victor, the Bishop of Rome in 190 AD, went further than Anicetus. He issued an ultimatum: any church that continued to observe Passover according to the biblical command would be excommunicated [^38.4]. This wasn’t about unity; it was about control.

Polycrates, a disciple of Polycarp, wouldn’t bow. In a letter to Rome, he listed the names of those who had faithfully kept the Passover, including John the Apostle, Philip, and, of course, Polycarp.

> “We observe the exact day; neither adding nor taking away. For in Asia, great luminaries have fallen asleep who shall rise again...These all kept the fourteenth day of the Passover according to the Gospel.” [^38.5]

He added, “I am not affrighted by terrifying words. For those greater than I have said, ‘We ought to obey God rather than men’” (Acts 5:29) [^38.5]. His words weren’t just defiance—they were a plea to remember what Jesus had established.

But Victor had already made up his mind.

For the first time in Christian history, a bishop of Rome tried to declare his authority over all the churches by threatening excommunication to anyone who kept the biblical Passover.

In 325 AD, the Council of Nicea settled the contention—Rome’s way.

And behind it all, a more subtle force was at work. A man named Eusebius begins rewriting the Church’s story. His influence would shape what many now call “Church history,” but his version told only one side.

### The Final Nail

Constantine. Part Christian. Part wolf in emperor’s clothing. Full politician. He was hailed by some and despised by others.

Though he legalized Christianity, he also helped to syncretize it with Roman paganism and politics.

He also financially enriched bishops. Three hundred of those same bishops answered his call for a worldwide ecumenical council at Nicaea. Allowing the ruler of Rome to initiate such a meeting became the beginning of the end for the Church as we know it.

Constantine’s intentions were evident when he declared:

> “Let us then have nothing in common with the detestable Jewish crowd.” [^38.6]

With the ink still wet on the Nicene Creed, he turned his attention to Passover. And with that, the Passover was severed from its biblical heritage.

Not simply forgotten.  
Not respectfully debated.  
Severed.

Passover was replaced with a new tradition tied to the Sunday after the spring equinox. This change was entirely detached from the sacred times that God instituted for His people.

But the most decisive move came not just in theology—but in time itself.

To facilitate this shift, a new calendar was adopted. The Gregorian calendar wasn’t merely a practical update; it ensured that Passover would never fall on the same day as the biblical feast. If it did, the celebration would be pushed forward by a week. As a result, the focus shifted from remembering His death to celebrating His resurrection.

Imagine forgetting your wedding anniversary and then celebrating it on a different day simply because it was more convenient or popular. To make up for forgetting the anniversary, you might try to pretend you’re remembering the first time you met. That’s akin to what happened with Passover. We didn’t just change the date; we lost its meaning.

In our church, there’s a family with a birthday tradition. On their birthday, that person gets to decide what everyone does for the whole day, and everyone happily goes along with it. Passover and God’s holy days are similar. They are special days that God has set apart. To honor these days, we celebrate them in the way He has asked us to.

At a certain point, the Church took it upon themselves to claim this day as their own to dictate. Those who clung to the traditional observances were branded as heretics and ultimately cast aside.

### What We Lost

Passover wasn’t a random ritual. It was the heartbeat of redemption—an appointed time that tied us to the Lamb’s sacrifice, a yearly reminder of God’s deliverance. Rome replaced it with something else: a Sunday celebration that morphed into weekly Communion, often stripped of its depth. 

Now? In most churches, we’ve swapped that for a cracker and a sip of juice, handed out in a rush between songs. Don’t get me wrong—Communion can still move us. But we’ve forgotten the story it’s meant to tell. We’ve also forgotten the calendar God entrusted—rich with prophetic meaning. Every step away from the biblical Passover was a step away from the simplicity Jesus modeled.

I’ve wrestled with this a lot over the years. No matter how intentional we tried to make Communion, it always felt like something critical was missing. When I saw the unbreakable connection to the holy day of Passover, that gap was finally closed. I will discuss this more at the end of the book and paint a picture of how this timeless celebration can be resurrected in the Church today.

The last reminder of why the biblical Passover is so crucial is found in Exodus 13:9: “It shall be as a sign on your hand and a memorial between your eyes.” The first mention of the Mark of God appears here as a bond with Passover never meant to be broken. 

Jesus didn’t abolish this prophetic feast—He fulfilled it. 

So why would we abolish it?

As Polycrates quoted the Apostles, “We ought to obey God rather than men. (Acts 5:29)” Why would we continue honoring the traditions of men above the truth of “the Gospel?” [^38.5]

The table is set. 
The invitation remains. 
Will you take your seat?

Will you return to how Jesus taught us to proclaim His death until He comes again?

## Appendix B: The Spring Feasts – Redemption in Bloom

> A lamb once slain, the blood once shed,  
> The leaven gone, the captives fled.  
> The grave unsealed, the Spirit poured—  
> Redemption’s rhythm is restored.

> “These are the appointed feasts of the Lord, holy convocations which you shall proclaim at the time appointed for them.” (Leviticus 23:4)

God doesn’t just speak through words—He speaks through time. Like Sabbath rest, the rhythms He established are holy invitations: gentle reminders to slow down, look back, and rediscover the story He wrote in blood and hope. These aren’t just rituals—they’re anniversaries, sacred appointments designed to renew our hearts in remembrance and ground us deeply in redemption.

Passover, Unleavened Bread, Firstfruits, Pentecost—each one fulfilled perfectly by Jesus, each one holding echoes of mercy, grace, and rescue. These spring feasts aren’t obligations or burdens. They’re invitations back to the table of communion—where a Lamb once slain offers again His body, His blood, and His promise to make all things new.

We’ve seen what happens when we abandon God’s rhythms—restlessness, compromise, and spiritual forgetfulness. But the story doesn’t have to end there. We can reclaim these lost feasts, not out of legalism, but longing. Just as the Sabbath anchors our week in rest, these appointed times anchor our year in redemption—reminding us that we’re part of something bigger, older, and richer than anything man-made.

What would happen if we embraced these ancient rhythms again—simply, humbly, intentionally? Could they stir our hearts to deeper remembrance, clearer purpose, and a sharper anticipation of what’s coming next? Let’s take a moment to rediscover the Spring Feasts—redemption in bloom—and see why they still matter deeply for those longing to return home.

#### Passover – The Blood on the Door

> “When I see the blood, I will pass over you.” (Exodus 12:13)

Passover was the beginning of everything. God told Israel to mark their calendars—this would be their first month, the start of their year. Why? Because this wasn’t just about escaping Egypt. It was about becoming His people. Saved. Set apart. Delivered by blood.

They were told to choose a spotless lamb, keep it for four days, then kill it at twilight. The blood would be applied to their doorposts. That night, judgment would pass through Egypt. The blood made the difference between death and life.

Jesus is our Passover Lamb. He entered Jerusalem on the tenth of the month, was examined and questioned just like the lamb, and was crucified at twilight on the 14th day—the exact day and time the lambs were slain.

John the Baptist knew who He was: _“Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!”_ (John 1:29)

> “Christ, our Passover, was sacrificed for us.” (1 Corinthians 5:7)

##### How We Can Remember

- Set aside a meal on the 14th day of the first biblical month (or as close as you can).
- Read the Exodus story aloud with family or friends.
- Reflect on the cross and share Communion slowly—remembering it was _on this day_ He gave His body and blood.
- Light a candle, break bread, pour wine or juice—and say, “This is our Passover. This is the Lamb.”

#### Unleavened Bread – Leaving Egypt Behind

> “Seven days you shall eat unleavened bread…for on this same day I have brought your armies out of the land of Egypt.” (Exodus 12:17)

After the lamb was slain, Israel left in haste. There was no time to wait for their bread to rise. God told them to remove all leaven—a symbol of corruption or sin—from their homes. For seven days, they were to eat only unleavened bread.

Leaven often represents sin, pride, or false teaching in Scripture. Jesus warned of the “leaven of the Pharisees,” and Paul told the Corinthians to “purge out the old leaven” (1 Corinthians 5:6–8).

Jesus, sinless and undefiled, was placed in the tomb just as the Feast of Unleavened Bread began. His body, the “bread of life,” was without leaven—holy and pure.

As His death broke the power of sin, this feast became a symbol of leaving corruption behind. The Exodus was more than physical escape. It was a call to sanctification.

##### How We Can Remember

- Take a week to fast from leavened bread or any food with yeast.
- Use this time to search your heart, asking the Holy Spirit to reveal hidden sin or compromise.
- Use the removal of leaven as a family object lesson: _What needs to go? What does holiness look like in our home?_
- Replace leavened bread with matzah (or simple flatbread) to remember the body that was broken for us.

#### Firstfruits – The Dawn of Resurrection

> “When you come into the land…you shall bring the sheaf of the firstfruits of your harvest to the priest.” (Leviticus 23:10)

On the morning after the Sabbath following Passover, Israel was commanded to bring the first sheaf of their barley harvest. It was not a holy convocation but a powerful symbol.

Two thousand years ago, on the morning after the Sabbath, as Israel presented the first sheaf to the priest, little did they know that the true Firstfruits was presenting Himself to the Father (John 20:17). Jesus fulfilled this in every way, conquering death and becoming the first harvest of resurrection.

> “But now Christ is risen from the dead, and has become the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep.” (1 Corinthians 15:20)

His resurrection wasn’t random. It fulfilled Firstfruits to the very day. He was lifted up—like the sheaf—and waved before the Father as the beginning of the great harvest. Because He rose, we will rise.

Firstfruits isn’t just about what Jesus did—it’s a promise of what’s coming. The grave is not the end. Resurrection is our hope.

#### Pentecost – Fire on the Mountain

> “And you shall count fifty days to the day after the seventh Sabbath…you shall have a holy convocation.” (Leviticus 23:16)

Fifty days after Firstfruits, Israel was to celebrate the Feast of Weeks—Shavuot, later known as Pentecost in Greek. It marked the wheat harvest and commemorated the giving of the Law at Sinai.

According to Jewish tradition, God gave the Law at Mount Sinai on this day—thunder, fire, and smoke descending on the mountain as God made covenant with His people.

Then, centuries later, on that same day—Pentecost—the Spirit was poured out.

> “When the day of Pentecost had fully come…there came a sound from heaven, as of a rushing mighty wind…” (Acts 2:1–2)

At Sinai, God gave stone tablets.  
In Jerusalem, He wrote His law on hearts.  
At Sinai, 3,000 died in judgment.  
At Pentecost, 3,000 were saved.

Pentecost is the marriage of fire and grace—the moment heaven empowered the Church not just to believe, but to become.

##### How We Can Remember

- Count the days from Firstfruits to Pentecost—make it a journey of reflection and anticipation.
- Gather for worship on the 50th day and pray for a fresh filling of the Holy Spirit.
- Read Acts 2 aloud—and ask: _Are we still walking in that same power today?_
- Set aside this day to seek God for revival in your heart, your home, and your church.

#### Why These Feasts Still Matter

Jesus fulfilled each of these feasts with divine precision.  
He was crucified on Passover.  
Buried during Unleavened Bread.  
Raised on Firstfruits.  
And the Spirit came on Pentecost.

These aren’t manmade traditions.  
They are God’s appointed times—_mo’edim_.  
They mark the rhythm of redemption.

And yet, most of the Church has forgotten them.

What happens when the Church forgets the calendar of God?  
We lose track of time.  
We lose our sense of urgency.  
We become desensitized to prophecy and dull to preparation.

But what if we remembered?

Not out of legalism, but longing.  
Not as a burden, but as a rhythm.  
Not to be bound to the past, but to live with purpose in the present.

These feasts aren’t required.  
But they are _invitations_—to come closer, remember more deeply, and anchor our lives in God’s unfolding story.

#### The Spring Rhythm

- Passover – _The Lamb is slain._
- Unleavened Bread – _Sin is buried._
- Firstfruits – _Resurrection begins._
- Pentecost – _The Spirit fills._

This is the rhythm of redemption.  
And it’s only the beginning.


## Appendix C: The Fall Feasts – Restoration and Return

> The trumpet sounds, the veil is torn,  
> The Judge arrives, the world is warned.  
> The King returns, His tent is spread—  
> And tears are dried where once they bled.
> 
> Come now, awake—these days proclaim:  
> He will return as He once came.

> “These are the Lord’s appointed feasts…a statute forever throughout your generations.” (Leviticus 23:37,41)

The Spring Feasts revealed the Lamb.  
The Fall Feasts reveal the Lion.

The first half of God’s calendar pointed to redemption—Jesus‘ death, resurrection, and the outpouring of the Spirit. These things have already happened, fulfilled in perfect detail.

But the second half—the Fall Feasts—remain prophetic. They point forward to events yet to come: the return of Christ, the judgment of the world, and the restoration of God’s presence among His people.

Jesus fulfilled the spring rehearsals _to the day._ Should we not expect the same precision for what’s still to come?

Let’s walk through the Fall Feasts—what they meant, how they point to the future, and how we can begin to remember them today.

#### Feast of Trumpets – The Day of the Awakening Blast

(Yom Teruah)

> “In the seventh month, on the first day…you shall have a sabbath-rest, a memorial of blowing of trumpets, a holy convocation.” (Leviticus 23:24)

The Feast of Trumpets is also known in Scripture and Jewish tradition as Yom Teruah—literally, _“The Day of the Awakening Blast.”_ It is not a quiet day. It’s the sound of heaven shaking the earth. A sound to wake up sleepers, warn the rebellious, and gather the faithful.

This feast is rich in prophetic parallels, especially regarding the return of the Messiah.

##### Symbols and Prophetic Meaning of the Feast of Trumpets

##### 1. The Feast When “No One Knows the Day or Hour”

This feast begins not on a full moon like Passover, but on a new moon—when the moon is dark and only a sliver of light returns. Because the new moon is difficult to predict precisely, ancient Israel would watch the skies. Witnesses had to confirm its sighting before the feast could begin.

Thus, it became known as “the feast of which no one knows the day or hour.”

This is the very language Jesus used when describing His return:

> _“But of that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels of heaven, but My Father only.”_ (Matthew 24:36)

This wasn’t a vague statement about complete unpredictability. It was likely a direct reference to this feast—an idiom His disciples would have understood. He was pointing them to a known rhythm on God’s calendar.


##### 2. The Awakening Blast – Call to Resurrection

One of the traditional names for the shofar blast on this day is the “Awakening Blast.”

Paul picks up this theme in his teaching on resurrection:

> _“Awake, you who sleep, arise from the dead, and Christ will give you light.”_ (Ephesians 5:14)  
> _“The trumpet will sound, and the dead will be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed.”_ (1 Corinthians 15:52)


##### 3. The Last Trump

Paul again uses Trumpets language:

> _“For the Lord Himself will descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of an archangel, and with the trumpet of God.”_  
> — 1 Thessalonians 4:16

In traditional Jewish observance, 100 trumpet blasts are sounded on Yom Teruah. The final one is called the Tekiah Gedolah—the _Great Trump_, the _Last Trumpet_. It is long, loud, and final.

So when Paul says _“at the last trumpet…”_, he wasn’t making up a new image. He was referring to a known and rehearsed moment—the very last trumpet of this feast.

Jesus said something similar in Matthew 24:

> _“And He will send His angels with a great sound of a trumpet, and they will gather together His elect...”_ (Matthew 24:31)

This is the gathering—another theme of the Feast of Trumpets. The sounding of the trumpet was used in Israel to assemble the people (Numbers 10:1–10), and prophetically, to gather the righteous from the ends of the earth.

##### 4. Trumpets in the Book of Revelation

The trumpet judgments in Revelation (chapters 8–11) also echo the Feast of Trumpets. While those trumpet blasts bring judgment, the final trumpet brings a declaration of victory:

> _“Then the seventh angel sounded: And there were loud voices in heaven, saying, ’The kingdoms of this world have become the kingdoms of our Lord and of His Christ…‘”_ (Revelation 11:15)

This seventh trumpet may correspond to the Great Trumpet—the climactic moment of the Feast of Trumpets.

##### 5. The Great Noise / Shout of God

Throughout Scripture, God’s return is marked by a great noise, shout, or trumpet:

- _“God has gone up with a shout, the Lord with the sound of a trumpet.”_ (Psalm 47:5)
- _“Blow the trumpet in Zion… for the day of the Lord is coming.”_ (Joel 2:1)
- _“The Lord will cause His glorious voice to be heard… with the flame of devouring fire, with scattering, tempest, and hailstones.”_ (Isaiah 30:30)
- _“The Lord will roar from Zion…”_ (Joel 3:16)

The trumpet is not quiet. It is a divine announcement. It shakes the earth. It awakens the dead. It signals the King.

##### Summary: What the Feast of Trumpets Teaches Us

- Watchfulness – Because no one knows the hour, we must be awake. (Matthew 25:13)
- Repentance – It begins the Ten Days of Awe, leading to Atonement.
- Resurrection – It points to the day the dead in Christ will rise.
- Return – It proclaims the arrival of the King.
- Gathering – It signals the assembly of the saints.
- Judgment – It begins the final phase of God’s dealings with the world.

#### Day of Atonement – Face to Face with God

(Yom Kippur)

> “It shall be to you a sabbath of solemn rest, and you shall afflict your souls…for it is the Day of Atonement, to make atonement for you before the Lord your God.” (Leviticus 23:27–28,32)

Yom Kippur was the holiest day of the year. On this one day, the high priest entered the Holy of Holies, offering blood for the sins of the nation. It was a day of deep humility, repentance, and awe.

Only one man, once a year, could draw near to God’s presence.

Until the veil was torn.

Jesus is our High Priest. He didn’t just enter the Holy of Holies—He became the atonement once for all. He doesn’t offer goats and bulls. He offered Himself. (Hebrews 9:11–14)

The Day of Atonement points to the final judgment—a time when every soul will stand before God. It also echoes the moment when Israel, in the last days, will finally recognize Yeshua as Messiah.

> “They will look on Me whom they pierced. Yes, they will mourn for Him…” (Zechariah 12:10)

Atonement is both personal and prophetic.  
It reminds us of the seriousness of sin, the holiness of God, and the hope of mercy.

##### How We Can Remember

- Set aside the day for prayer, fasting, and repentance.
- Read Hebrews 9–10 and reflect on Jesus as your eternal High Priest.
- Ask God to search your heart and cleanse you deeply (Psalm 139:23–24).
- Pray for Israel—that the veil would be lifted and many would see the Messiah.


#### Feast of Tabernacles – God With Us Again

(Sukkot)

> “You shall dwell in booths for seven days…that your generations may know that I made the children of Israel dwell in booths when I brought them out of the land of Egypt.” (Leviticus 23:42–43)

Tabernacles was a feast of joy and remembrance. God told Israel to live in temporary shelters for seven days, celebrating how He protected them in the wilderness. It was a reminder of dependence—and of His presence among them.

Sukkot is a prophetic picture of the Messiah dwelling with us.

> “And the Word became flesh and dwelt [tabernacled] among us.” (John 1:14)

But this feast also looks forward. One day, Jesus will return to reign, and God will dwell with His people forever.

> “Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men…” (Revelation 21:3)

Tabernacles points to the Millennial Kingdom—the time when the King will set up His throne on earth. Zechariah even tells us that all nations will keep the Feast of Tabernacles in the future (Zechariah 14:16–19).

This isn’t just memory. It’s prophecy.

##### How We Can Remember

- Build a simple tent, booth, or even a fort—indoors or outdoors—and spend time there.
- Share meals under the sky, read Scripture, and tell stories of God’s faithfulness.
- Invite friends or neighbors for a feast. Celebrate joyfully—this was meant to be the most joyful feast of all.
- Reflect on what it means to dwell with God now, and long for the day He dwells with us fully.



#### The Last Great Day – New Beginning

> “On the eighth day you shall have a holy convocation…It is a sacred assembly, and you shall do no customary work.” (Leviticus 23:36)

The Feast of Tabernacles lasted seven days, but God commanded an eighth day—a special final celebration. This is known as Shemini Atzeret, the “Last Great Day.”

Eight is the number of new beginnings.  
This day doesn’t point back.  
It points forward—to something eternal.

> “On the last and greatest day of the feast, Jesus stood and said…’If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink.‘” (John 7:37)

Jesus offered living water on this day—a hint of eternal life.

The Last Great Day may point to the New Heaven and New Earth—the day after history ends and eternity begins. It’s a vision of rest that never ends, joy that never fades, and communion that never breaks.

##### How We Can Remember

- End the feast with quiet joy and expectation.
- Reflect on Revelation 21–22 and imagine eternity with God.
- Share a final meal with loved ones, symbolizing the marriage supper of the Lamb.
- Declare: _“The best is yet to come.”_

#### Why the Fall Feasts Still Matter

These Fall Feasts are not burdens—they are blessings. They anchor our hearts in prophetic expectancy, tuning us to the rhythms of heaven rather than the noise of the world. They call us to awaken, to repent, to remember that this story isn’t over. The King is coming again.

Yet tragically, the Church has largely forgotten these holy days and replaced them with something lesser—something human-made, compromised, and hollow. Instead of trumpets, we’ve settled for traditions. Instead of repentance, we’ve embraced rituals emptied of their power.

It’s time to ask ourselves: Whose calendar have we been keeping? Whose rhythms have we followed?

Let’s look honestly at how we traded God’s brilliant lights—His appointed times—for dim substitutes created by human hands. Let’s rediscover what we lost, why it matters, and how we can reclaim the sacred days that keep our souls ready and our eyes fixed firmly on the coming King.

## Appendix E: Addressing Common Objections to the Sabbath

If you’re unsure about the Sabbath, you’re not alone. I’ve been there. Here’s what helped me sort through the objections.

This chapter is for those who still have questions regarding the relevance of the Sabbath. If you are still unconvinced about the Sabbath regarding the question “To keep or not to keep?”, this chapter is meant as a primer to help you continue exploring the question for yourself. There is much I could discuss—entire books have been written on the subject—but here are some common contentions and some good food for thought to get you going if you want to understand this topic thoroughly. 

### The Sabbath is a Test

I talked about the eternal and universal scope of God’s Law in Part Two. His moral standards were not invented at Mount Sinai. They were inextricably tied to the Old Covenant, although they were still the bedrock of that covenant.

Another clear example of the Law’s existence before Sinai is the Sabbath. In Exodus 16:23-30, God rebuked Israel for breaking the Sabbath when they attempted to gather manna on the seventh day. This occurred before the Ten Commandments were audibly delivered at Mount Sinai (Exodus 20). 

The Sabbath commandment in Exodus 20:8-11 explicitly ties the observance of this day to Creation itself:  “For in six days the Lord made the heavens and the earth, the sea, and all that is in them, and rested the seventh day. Therefore the Lord blessed the Sabbath day and hallowed it.”

This passage reminds us that the Sabbath is not merely a Jewish tradition but a memorial of creation, instituted at the very beginning of human history (Genesis 2:2-3). God’s judgment on Israel for disregarding the Sabbath in Exodus 16 underscores its expectation as a divine Law before it was codified at Sinai.

Because of Exodus 16:23-30 and Genesis 26:5, rabbinic teaching has long maintained that Abraham, among others, guarded the seventh day as holy. 

But it’s interesting to me that in Exodus 16:4, God says He will test His people “...whether they will walk in My law or not.” There are a dozen Laws He could have chosen to test Israel in, but He chose the Sabbath. Ironically, the one law they likely could not keep as slaves in Egypt, and you would think you would be eager to celebrate as free people.

God sent a double portion of manna on the sixth day, and when the people still went to gather on the seventh day, God said, “And the Lord said to Moses, ‘How long do you refuse to keep My commandments and My laws? See! For the Lord has given you the Sabbath...” (Exodus 16:28-29)

### The Critical Silence on the Sabbath

The Council of Jerusalem (Acts 15) was the defining moment in church history where the apostles settled the question of how the Mosaic Law applied to Gentile believers. The council addressed the teachings of Judaizers, who insisted that Gentiles must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to be saved. The Law of God was never in question. 

The apostles, led by Peter and James, ruled that Gentiles were only required to observe Mosaic laws regarding idolatry, sexual immorality, blood, and strangled animals. There were additional laws given to Moses which further defined idolatry and sexual immorality such as bestiality and homosexuality. Those acts were considered immoral for all believers.

But notice what the council did not address: _There was no command to change the Sabbath or establish Sunday as the Lord’s Day._ Just like there was no mention about taking God’s name in vain or committing murder, those moral laws were implicitly understood to be binding upon all believers.

But, if ever there was a time for the apostles to clarify that the seventh-day Sabbath was no longer binding—or to introduce a new day of worship—this would have been it. Their silence on this issue should be deafening, but still some want to excuse the verdict of this council or move along and point to other scriptures as evidence.

### The Sabbath Remains

Explain the irony that some people use Hebrews 4 to say that the sabbath is now a spiritual rest, “Jesus is my rest”, when Hebrews 4:9 is one of the clearest scriptures in the NT when not translated improperly. It might be worth questioning the intention of translators who take some much liberty with this word that can only mean (“sabbatismos” = literal Sabbath-keeping).

This chapter is saying “don’t be disobedient.” And also a bit of an oxymoron, “Make every **effort** to enter into that **rest**”. 

### The Lord’s Day?

Ask most Christians why they worship on Sunday, and you’ll hear, “Because Jesus rose on Sunday,” or “It’s the Lord’s Day.” I get it—it’s what I was taught too. It’s like a tradition so old we assume it’s always been that way.

In Revelation 1:10 John says, “I was in the Spirit on the Lord’s Day.” Sunday, right? Not so fast. The text never says that.  

Look at who wrote it: John, a Jew taught by Jesus, knew Him as “Lord of the Sabbath” (Matthew 12:8). In Isaiah 58:13, God calls the Sabbath “My holy day...the holy day of the LORD...” That’s Saturday. So why assume John meant Sunday? Where’s the proof?

There isn’t any. 

Sunday as the Lord’s Day? That idea grew later, fueled by the misconception that Jesus rose on Sunday. The Epistle of Barnabas hints at it, and Ignatius ran with it, but it’s not in the Bible.  
 
Even if Jesus did rise on Sunday, where’s the command to alter or abolish the Sabbath? Nowhere. God blessed the seventh day (Genesis 2:3). Jesus kept it. And of all people, Paul did too. Even after the resurrection, Gentiles begged Paul to preach “the next Sabbath”—not Sunday, and Paul gave no correction.[^39.1]

Eusebius was the first to argue that Acts 20:7 and 1 Corinthians 16:2 prove that the Church replaced Saturday with Sunday. But look closer—these verses don’t hold up that claim. 

Acts 20:7 mentions a gathering on “the first day of the week,” which, in Jewish time, starts Saturday at sunset. This event describes a dinner teaching time after the Sabbath day ends. Paul taught late into the night, not to establish a new worship day but because he was leaving the next day. It was a goodbye, not a new precedent.

Then there’s 1 Corinthians 16:2, where Paul asks believers to set aside offerings on the first day. This wasn’t a church service but a pragmatic process collecting the offering. There was no Sunday worship, no Sabbath replacement—just everyday life.

It’s important to note that even if the previous verses described a worship gathering, that would not have nullified the day God blessed and made holy. Such a change would have to have been authorized by Jesus or His apostles, not altered through mere practice or tradition. 

Also, Acts 2:46 describes believers meeting daily for fellowship and breaking bread. If the argument is that Sunday replaced the Sabbath simply because believers gathered that day, then by that logic, every day of the week became a Sabbath.
### The Progressive Perversion

Three lies crept in:

- Some traded it for Sunday, running from anything “Jewish.”
- Others watered it down, draining all practical meaning.
- A cousin to the second lie, all the commandments were made obsolete (I addressed this in Part One)

All these lies miss the Mark. Most of us either honor the wrong day or shrug off the Sabbath completely. And in the shuffle, we’ve lost a blessing God meant for us all along. Stick with me. We’re diving into why ancient sun worshippers dictated the day, why others have ditched the Sabbath entirely, and why I think God’s Word begs us to reconsider.

The shift didn’t happen overnight; it slowly faded over centuries. The reason for this is the same that led to the passing of Passover: mixed motives and bad theology. 

The first recorded push to replace the Sabbath with Sunday came from Ignatius of Antioch, writing around 110 AD, just decades after the apostles.

> “Let every friend of Christ keep the Lord’s Day as a festival, the resurrection-day, the queen and chief of all the days of the week.”[^3]

It’s a striking claim, but where did it come from? It feels like a weed suddenly sprouting in a healthy garden—unexplained and growing taller each century.

Maybe Ignatius misread Paul’s letters, seeing freedom from legalism as a license to abandon the Sabbath. Or perhaps he drew from the anti-Semitic Epistle of Barnabas, which scorned Jewish practices. But I suspect something else drove him: a longing to stand apart. 

After Rome crushed Jerusalem in 70 AD, being Jewish—or even Jewish-adjacent—was a liability. Christians faced a choice: blend in or stand out. Ignatius chose distinction—not just to avoid persecution, but to give Christianity its own identity, separate from its Jewish roots.  

And it worked. 

Sunday became a marker, a way to say, “We’re not them.” But at what cost? Ignatius planted a seed that Rome would water—a seed that distanced us from the day God blessed. Was it hostility toward Judaism? Maybe not outright. But it was a compromise, born of human invention, not divine command.

By the mid-second century, a new voice emerged—Justin Martyr.
Justin didn’t appeal to biblical commandment. Instead, he pointed to creation and Christ’s resurrection—ideas that were more palatable to the Greek and Roman mind. Yet, Justin’s argument for Sunday worship wasn’t his only view on the Sabbath. He also taught that the Sabbath was a punishment placed on the Jews for their sins. [^4] 

Others scoffed at the Sabbath, treating it as little more than a lazy man’s excuse. [^5] Then came Constantine. In 321 AD, he legalized a new sabbath on “the venerable day of the Sun.” [^6] Notice that he didn’t just call it Sunday—this was more than a nod to the worship of Sol Invictus, the Unconquered Sun. By legally mandating Sunday as a day of rest, Constantine created a unifying rhythm for his empire, ensuring that pagans and Christians could find common ground.

Eusebius, as we’ve already seen, helped spin Constantine’s Sunday decree as divine will—even while most churches still honored the Sabbath. [^7]

Greek philosophy also heavily influenced Christian thought—especially Platonism, which emphasized the spiritual over the physical. Origen called the Sabbath childish, reducing it to a metaphor of spiritual rest, not an actual day. [^8]

But what does a metaphorical Sabbath even mean?

While it sounds profound, it ignores the simple reality that humans still need rest.

### Colossians 2: A Debate Over Observing the Feast Days

> “So let no one judge you in food or in drink, or regarding a festival or a new moon or sabbaths, which are a shadow of things to come, but the substance is of Christ.” (Colossians 2:16-17)

This passage is one of the most commonly cited to argue against the Sabbath. At first glance, it seems to dismiss the Sabbath as irrelevant, a shadow of something greater. But is that really what Paul is saying? Let’s take a closer look.

In this letter to the Colossians, Paul is addressing a specific issue: false teachers who were pressuring believers to follow strict rules tied to food, drink, festivals, and ceremonial laws. These were part of the broader system of Jewish holy days, which included annual festivals, new moon celebrations, and additional ceremonial sabbaths. These ceremonial practices were part of the sacrificial system outlined in the Torah, pointing forward to Christ as their fulfillment.

It’s important to note that the word “sabbaths” here is plural. This plural usage often refers to ceremonial sabbaths tied to specific feast days, such as the Day of Atonement or the Feast of Trumpets. These were different from the weekly Sabbath established at Creation and reaffirmed in the Ten Commandments. The weekly Sabbath is rooted in God’s eternal moral law, while the ceremonial sabbaths were part of the temporary laws given through Moses.

The context makes this distinction clear. Paul mentions “food or drink, or regarding a festival or a new moon or sabbaths”—terms that align closely with the ceremonial practices of the Mosaic Law. These laws included detailed guidelines for offerings, travel, and rituals, many of which were burdensome to Gentile believers who had no cultural connection to them. Paul’s message here wasn’t about rejecting the moral Sabbath of the Ten Commandments but rather freeing believers from the obligation to observe the ceremonial aspects of the law, which had been fulfilled in Christ.

Paul’s emphasis is that these ceremonial practices were a shadow pointing forward to Christ. Once the reality—the “substance”—arrived, the shadow was no longer necessary. But notice what Paul doesn’t say: he doesn’t call the Sabbath a burden or declare it abolished. The weekly Sabbath, established long before Moses, remains a part of God’s eternal design for humanity.

### Romans 14: A Debate Over Observing Fasting Days

> “One person esteems one day above another; another esteems every day alike. Let each be fully convinced in his own mind.” (Romans 14:5)

This verse is often misunderstood as a statement against the Sabbath. On the surface, it might seem like Paul is saying, “It doesn’t matter which day you worship—just pick one.” But that’s not the context of Romans 14. The issue here concerns personal convictions regarding fasting.

The early church was navigating significant cultural tensions. Jewish believers were accustomed to observing traditional fasts and holy days, while Gentile believers had no such background. Some believers continued to fast on specific days or observe certain traditions, while others felt no obligation to do so.

 Paul is calling for unity in the church, urging believers not to judge one another over these non-essential issues. He isn’t saying that God’s commandments, like the Sabbath, are now optional.

### The Sabbath is for The Jews

In Mark 2:27-28, Jesus said, “The Sabbath was made for man, and not man for the Sabbath. Therefore, the Son of Man is also Lord of the Sabbath.” This thought reflects some of the modern Jewish thinking at the time that has been recorded in Pirkei Avot 6:2:

> “Rabbi Shimon ben Menasiah said: The Sabbath was given to you, not you to the Sabbath.”

Notice what Jesus didn’t say. He didn’t say, “The Sabbath was made for the Jews.” He said it was made for man—for humanity. From the very beginning, God designed the Sabbath for everyone.

We first see this in Genesis 2:2-3, where God sanctified the seventh day and rested. This was before Abraham, before Moses, before the Ten Commandments. The Sabbath wasn’t a law given exclusively to Israel—it was woven into the fabric of Creation itself. It was God’s way of teaching us to trust Him, to rest in His provision, and to remember that He is our Creator.

“One can only say with certainty that already in the pre-exilic period the Sabbath was kept at the end of every week as a day of rest ordained and sanctified by Yahweh.”

The Commandment is for everyone, “your animals, your guests”. It was commanded to foreigners passing through.

### Yeshua’s Fulfillment of the Law

> “Do not think that I came to destroy the Law or the Prophets. I did not come to destroy but to fulfill. For assuredly, I say to you, till heaven and earth pass away, one jot or one tittle will by no means pass from the law till all is fulfilled. Whoever therefore breaks one of the least of these commandments, and teaches men so, shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven; but whoever does and teaches them, he shall be called great in the kingdom of heaven.” (Matthew 5:17-19)

For years, I misunderstood what Yeshua meant by “fulfilling” the law. Like many, I assumed it meant the law was done away with—finished, no longer relevant for followers of Christ. But if that were true, why would Yeshua explicitly say He came not to destroy the law? Why would He warn us that breaking even the smallest commandment matters in the kingdom of heaven?

It’s clear now that Yeshua’s words were never about abolishing the law, but about bringing it to its fullest expression. Fulfillment doesn’t mean cancellation—it means completion. Jesus showed us the true depth of the law, not just in outward observance, but from the heart.

Think about His teaching in the Sermon on the Mount. Yeshua didn’t discard Commandments like “You shall not murder” or “You shall not commit adultery.” Instead, He raised the standard: anger was equated with murder, and lust with adultery. Fulfillment, in this sense, means He deepened our understanding of the law, revealing its moral and spiritual intent.

### Fulfillment and the Sabbath

This same principle applies to the Sabbath. Some have argued that Yeshua’s fulfillment of the law abolished the Sabbath commandment. Throughout His ministry, Yeshua observed the Sabbath faithfully. He taught in the synagogues on the Sabbath (Luke 4:16), healed the sick on the Sabbath (Mark 3:1-5), and called His followers to rediscover its purpose. His actions didn’t diminish the Sabbath’s significance—they restored it.

However, the Pharisees often accused Yeshua of breaking the Sabbath. But what they were really defending were their own rules—legalistic traditions they had added to God’s law. These rules, hundreds of them, turned the Sabbath into a burden rather than the blessing God intended it to be.

When Jesus allowed His disciples to pluck grain on the Sabbath or healed a man with a withered hand, He was not breaking the Sabbath. Instead, He was reclaiming it. “The Sabbath was made for man, and not man for the Sabbath,” He declared (Mark 2:27). Yeshua reminded us that the Sabbath is a gift, not a yoke of bondage. It’s a day for doing good, for restoring life, and for resting from our work as God rested from His.

Imagine this for a moment: If Jesus had truly broken the Sabbath, He would have sinned. If He sinned, He could not have been the spotless Lamb of God. And if He wasn’t the spotless Lamb, we would still be in our sins. The very fact that Yeshua is our perfect Savior proves that He upheld the Sabbath in both spirit and truth.

### The Heart of Sabbath Observance

What Yeshua rejected was not the Sabbath itself, but the legalism surrounding it. The Pharisees had taken what was meant to be a day of blessing and turned it into a day of burden. By healing on the Sabbath, Jesus demonstrated what Sabbath-keeping looks like in its purest form: a day dedicated to God, full of mercy, restoration, and rest.

Yeshua never taught that the Sabbath was abolished. If anything, He taught us how to observe it with the right heart. The Sabbath is a commandment, yes—but it’s also an invitation. When I reflect on Yeshua’s ministry, I see the beauty of the Sabbath more clearly. He didn’t come to remove the Sabbath from our lives—He came to show us its true purpose. He fulfilled the law by embodying its heart: love for God and love for others. And the Sabbath is an expression of both.

> “For the Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath.” (Matthew 12:8)

If Jesus is Lord of the Sabbath, should we not submit ourselves to what He is Lord over? Should we not honor what He honored, rest in what He rested, and trust in what He trusted? The Sabbath is not just a day—it’s a declaration of faith in the One who created it.

### Hebrews 4:9

Some argue that the Sabbath was abolished or changed after the Cross, but Scripture never says that. Instead, we find evidence of its enduring significance. Hebrews 4:9 uses the Greek word sabbatismos to describe the “rest” that remains for the people of God. This word doesn’t mean a vague spiritual rest; it literally means “Sabbath-keeping.” And this passage warns us not to fall into the same disobedience as those who ignored God’s commands in the wilderness.

### Conclusion

> “Therefore the Son of Man is also Lord of the Sabbath.” (Mark 2:28)

The Sabbath is not merely a rule to observe but a relationship to embrace, a weekly declaration that He is our Creator, Provider, and Rest.

After exploring the arguments often raised against the Sabbath, one thing becomes clear: this day was never intended to be a burden. It was meant to be a blessing. The heart of the Sabbath is not legalism; it is love. It calls us to step away from the noise, to set aside our striving, and to remember who we belong to.

The objections we’ve examined—whether rooted in Colossians 2, Romans 14, or misunderstandings of Yeshua’s teachings—often come from a place of confusion, not clarity. When we dig deeper into Scripture, the Sabbath emerges not as an outdated relic but as a timeless gift, woven into Creation itself and reaffirmed by Christ.

I remember my life before observing the Sabbath. My life was not marked by qualities I’d be proud to herald. Anxiety, restlessness, striving, and discontentment are the words that come to mind when I think about who I was before I made that holy day holy. As I’ve sought what the Sabbath means in my life, I’ve discovered that it’s less about what I give up and more about what I gain. 

If you’ve wrestled with the idea of keeping the Sabbath, I encourage you to approach it as a new opportunity. What might change in your life if you embraced this day, not out of obligation but out of love for the One who created it?

The Sabbath is more than a Commandment—it’s a sign. It points us back to God, anchoring us in His eternal rhythm of rest and worship. It’s a weekly act of trust, a declaration that our identity is found not in what we do but in who we are as His people.

> “Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.” (Matthew 11:28)

This rest that Jesus offers is at the heart of the Sabbath. The invitation of Christ is to find renewal in Him and step away from the restless striving of the world.

So, the question isn’t simply, “To keep or not to keep?” The Sabbath is a gift waiting to be unwrapped. Maybe the more profound question would be, “What does it say if you reject this gift?” Finally, a question I want you to consider is this: Could this rhythm of rest be the gift your soul has been longing for?

## Appendix F: Rewriting Church History

This appendix provides historical support and documentation for several claims made in Chapter 18 (_The Passing of Passover_) and Chapter 19 (_The Sabbath Forgotten_). The goal is to show that key changes to early Christian doctrine and practice—particularly around Passover, the Sabbath, and church structure—were not rooted in apostolic teaching, but instead developed in the second to fourth centuries, often under the influence of Roman politics and theology.

While some of these shifts are attributed to early figures like Ignatius of Antioch and Justin Martyr, the most decisive moment came through Eusebius of Caesarea, a fourth-century bishop and historian whose writings have heavily influenced how we understand early church history.

### 1. Who Was Eusebius of Caesarea?

Eusebius (c. 260–340 AD) was the bishop of Caesarea and is often called the “Father of Church History” because of his 10-volume work, _Ecclesiastical History_, which chronicled Christianity from the time of the apostles to Constantine.

But Eusebius was not a neutral historian.

He was a personal ally and theological advisor to Emperor Constantine. He helped shape the outcome of the Council of Nicaea (AD 325), where imperial influence guided many ecclesiastical decisions, including the rejection of the biblical Passover date in favor of Easter Sunday.

Eusebius was also the author of _Life of Constantine_, a highly favorable biography that reads more like imperial propaganda than objective history. In it, he praises Constantine not only as a ruler but as a quasi-divine agent of God’s will.

In short, Eusebius was both a clerical insider and a political apologist, whose version of church history strongly aligned with the interests of imperial Christianity.

---

### 2. Eusebius‘ Role in Preserving and Promoting Ignatius’ Letters

The earliest explicit mention of Ignatius’ letters comes from Eusebius himself, in _Ecclesiastical History_ (Book 3, Chapter 36), where he lists seven letters as authentic:

> “We know of his [Ignatius’] epistles... which he sent to the churches while on the road to martyrdom from Syria to Rome, and especially to the church in Rome, warning the brethren there not to attempt to secure his release from martyrdom.”  
> — _Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History_, 3.36

These seven letters—known today as the “short recension”—are the only ones that most scholars consider potentially authentic. However, even these were only preserved through later manuscripts, many of which contain theological language that seems anachronistic for the early second century.

#### What’s Missing?

No undisputed references to Ignatius‘ letters exist in any surviving Christian writings prior to Eusebius. This includes:

- Irenaeus (c. 130–202 AD)
    
- Tertullian (c. 155–220 AD)
    
- Clement of Alexandria (c. 150–215 AD)
    
- Origen (c. 185–253 AD)
    

While some scholars argue that there may be “possible allusions” in Irenaeus or Origen, these are speculative and lack clear attribution. The absence of direct quotations or naming of Ignatius is striking, especially considering how often other early Christian writings quote or refer to Paul, Peter, or John.

> Most notably, when Justin Martyr later describes the Eucharist and Sunday worship in _First Apology_ (c. 155 AD), he never mentions Ignatius—even though his theology and terminology are similar.

This silence is striking. J.B. Lightfoot, a staunch defender of Ignatius’ authenticity, acknowledges this:

> “There is no trace of acquaintance with the Ignatian epistles in the writings of Justin Martyr... This silence is certainly a difficulty.”  
> — J.B. Lightfoot, _The Apostolic Fathers_, Vol. II, Part I: _S. Ignatius_, Macmillan, 1889, p. 64

Had Ignatius’ letters been widely known and accepted in Justin’s time, it is highly likely Justin would have appealed to them—especially since he was writing in Rome, where Ignatius was martyred.

This suggests the Ignatian letters may not have circulated broadly—or may have been edited or compiled after Justin’s time and before Eusebius’.

Conclusion: The sudden appearance of Ignatius’ letters in Eusebius’ work—especially at a time when their content supported the emerging Roman theological system—is suspicious and calls their authenticity and editorial integrity into question.

---

### 3. Anachronistic Theology in the Ignatian Letters

The short recension of Ignatius’ letters includes several doctrines that appear fully developed, despite being absent from earlier Christian writings:

#### A. Sunday Worship

> “Let every friend of Christ keep the Lord’s Day as a festival, the resurrection-day, the queen and chief of all the days of the week.”  
> — _Epistle to the Magnesians_, 9:1

This statement promotes Sunday as a replacement for the Sabbath—yet no such instruction exists in Scripture or earlier first-century texts.

#### B. “Real Presence” in the Eucharist

> “They abstain from the Eucharist and from prayer because they do not confess that the Eucharist is the flesh of our Savior Jesus Christ...”  
> — _Epistle to the Smyrnaeans_, 7:1

This sounds like later sacramental theology. The terminology and concept of real presence are absent from Scripture, the _Didache_, and early apostolic writings.

#### C. Strong Hierarchical Church Structure

> “Where the bishop is, there is the Church.”  
> — _Epistle to the Smyrnaeans_, 8:2

This reflects a growing emphasis on episcopal authority, consistent with what Constantine and Eusebius later promoted.

These ideas, while presented as early tradition, all align perfectly with the structure and doctrines of the fourth-century Roman Church.

---

### 4. Polycrates of Ephesus: An Early Opposing Voice

In contrast, Polycrates of Ephesus, writing around 190 AD, presents a different tradition—one rooted in the biblical Passover and tied directly to the apostolic witness of John, Philip, and Polycarp.

> “We observe the exact day; neither adding nor taking away. For in Asia also great lights have fallen asleep… such as Philip… and John, who was both a witness and a teacher… All these kept the fourteenth day of the Passover.”  
> — _Polycrates, Letter to Victor_ (quoted in _Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History_ 5.24)

Polycrates’ testimony stands in direct opposition to what Eusebius would later promote. His letter shows:

- Continued observance of the biblical Passover (Nisan 14)
    
- A direct appeal to apostolic tradition
    
- A refusal to submit to Rome’s authority
    

#### Why is this important?

Polycrates’ letter was likely circulated among many churches and represents a collective stance of Asian bishops. This makes it far harder to alter or suppress. It is a primary witness to a strand of early Christianity that resisted Roman innovation.

---

### 5. Socrates Scholasticus and the Sabbath Question

Fast forward two centuries: Socrates Scholasticus, a fifth-century church historian and successor to Eusebius‘ historical work, gives us this remarkable insight:

> “Although almost all churches throughout the world celebrate the sacred mysteries on the Sabbath of every week, yet the Christians of Alexandria and Rome, on account of some ancient tradition, have ceased to do this.”  
> — _Ecclesiastical History_, Book 5, Chapter 22

This is decades after Constantine and Eusebius, and yet the majority of churches were still keeping the Sabbath—not Sunday. Rome and Alexandria were the exceptions.

This historical note flatly contradicts the impression given by Eusebius that Sunday worship was universal and apostolic from the beginning.

---

### 6. The Calendar Shift and the Loss of Passover

At the Council of Nicaea (AD 325), Constantine—with Eusebius’ support—declared that Passover should no longer be kept according to the Jewish calendar, but instead be celebrated on Sunday, following the Roman practice of Easter.

Constantine’s words are chilling in their disdain:

> “Let us then have nothing in common with the detestable Jewish crowd...”  
> — _Eusebius, Life of Constantine_, 3.18

After Nicaea, Roman authorities continued to separate Christian practice from its Jewish roots—abandoning not only the calendar, but the entire biblical framework of worship.

---

### 7. Why This Matters

If Ignatius’ letters were edited—or even fabricated—to align with Eusebius’ theology, then doctrines like:

- Sunday worship
    
- The sacramental Eucharist
    
- Centralized episcopal authority
    

…are not apostolic, but post-apostolic innovations.

Likewise, if the earliest testimony of church practice (from figures like Polycrates and Socrates Scholasticus) contradicts Eusebius’ narrative, then we are forced to re-examine everything we think we know about early Christianity.

---

### Final Thoughts

Eusebius wrote history to serve the empire. His version of Christianity emphasized unity through conformity and favored Roman theology over apostolic fidelity.

Thankfully, other voices were preserved—voices like Polycrates, who reminded us to “obey God rather than men.”

Scripture is still our standard. And when tradition—however ancient—contradicts the Word, it must be questioned.

Let the reader test all things and hold fast to what is good.

## Appendix G: The Missing Words That Matter

> “This kind does not come out except by prayer and fasting.” (Matthew 17:21)

If you open most modern translations, you won’t find Matthew 17:21 in the text at all. It’s relegated to a footnote, if it appears at all. In Mark 9:29, the parallel passage, many translations remove the words “and fasting,” leaving only “prayer.” The footnotes typically say something like: “Some manuscripts include the words ‘and fasting.’”

That sounds reasonable enough. After all, we want to be faithful to the most reliable manuscripts, don’t we?

But there’s more to this story. And it matters far more than most people realize.

### The Context Demands It

Let’s start with the obvious problem: **the context doesn’t make sense without those words.**

The disciples had already been given authority to cast out demons. Jesus had sent them out two by two, and they returned rejoicing because even the demons were subject to them in His name (Luke 10:17). They had been healing the sick and casting out demons successfully. This wasn’t their first rodeo.

So when they failed to cast out this particular demon, and Jesus said, “This kind does not come out except by prayer,” are we really supposed to believe the disciples simply forgot to pray? That they faced a demon-possessed boy, attempted an exorcism, and never once uttered a prayer?

That’s absurd.

The disciples prayed. Of course they prayed. They had seen Jesus pray. They had asked Him to teach them to pray. Prayer was fundamental to everything they did.

So what was different this time? What was missing?

**Fasting.**

That’s the only explanation that makes theological, logical, and contextual sense. Jesus wasn’t saying they needed to pray—they already knew that. He was revealing a deeper level of spiritual warfare, one that required not just prayer, but the combination of prayer and fasting.

### The Biblical Precedent`

This isn’t a new concept. It goes back to the prophets.

Consider Daniel’s vision in Daniel 10. The angel appeared to him and said:

> “Do not be afraid, Daniel, for from the first day that you set your heart to understand, and to humble yourself before your God, your words were heard; and I have come because of your words. But the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days; and behold, Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me.” (Daniel 10:12–13)

Think about that. God heard Daniel’s prayer **immediately**. The angel was dispatched **the first day**. But the answer was delayed for twenty-one days because of spiritual warfare in the heavenly realms.

And what was Daniel doing during those twenty-one days? 

**Fasting.**

> “In those days I, Daniel, was mourning three full weeks. I ate no pleasant food, no meat or wine came into my mouth, nor did I anoint myself at all, till three whole weeks were fulfilled.” (Daniel 10:2–3)

Daniel’s persistent fasting coincided with the breakthrough in the spiritual realm. The angel was finally able to break through the demonic resistance and bring God’s message.

This is the pattern: when facing extraordinary spiritual opposition, **prayer and fasting** work together to break through the darkness.

### The Manuscript Evidence

Now let’s address the textual criticism argument, because this is where things get interesting.

When your Bible’s footnote says “some manuscripts include the words ‘and fasting,’” it gives the impression that maybe a handful of later, less reliable copies added these words. But that’s not what “some” means in this context.

**By “some manuscripts,” they mean virtually all of them.**

There are over 4,000 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament. And the vast majority of them—the Byzantine manuscripts, the Latin Vulgate, the Syriac versions, the Coptic versions, spanning different centuries and geographical regions—all include the words “and fasting.”

How many manuscripts omit these words?

**Two.**

The two oldest manuscripts we have: Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus.

Now, I’m not a manuscript scholar. I respect the work of textual criticism. But let’s be honest about what’s happening here: we’re being asked to believe that two manuscripts outweigh the testimony of thousands.

### The Critical Text Controversy

The reason modern translations omit these words traces back to the work of Westcott and Hort in the late 1800s. They established a principle that became foundational to modern textual criticism: **when manuscripts disagree, the oldest manuscripts should prevail.**

That’s a generally sound rule. Older copies are, in theory, closer to the original and less likely to have accumulated scribal errors or intentional changes.

But there are problems with applying this principle universally, especially in this case:

**1. The Theological Logic Demands the Words**

As we’ve already established, the passage doesn’t make sense without “and fasting.” If the disciples had already been successfully casting out demons through prayer, why would Jesus tell them they failed because they didn’t pray? The only logical explanation is that He was introducing a new element: fasting.

**2. The Manuscript Tradition Is Too Consistent**

If “and fasting” was added by later scribes, we’re talking about a remarkably coordinated effort. These words appear in manuscripts from different centuries, different geographical regions, different language traditions—all supposedly adding the exact same words in the exact same place.

That’s not how textual corruption typically works. Scribal errors tend to be random, localized, and diverse. But here we have a uniform tradition across time and geography, all including these words.

The simpler explanation? **The words were original, and two early manuscripts omitted them.** Two manuscripts that both originated in Rome and Alexandria.

**3. Questions About the Critical Text Itself**

Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus, while old, are not without their own problems. Codex Sinaiticus, for instance, shows evidence of multiple correctors and thousands of alterations. Codex Vaticanus was discovered buried in the Vatican archive. Codex Sinaiticus was found in a trash heap behind a monastery. Quite astounding considering the monetary value of these manuscripts alone. These manuscripts, while valuable, are not infallible.

**4. Older Manuscripts Have Been Found**

Since Westcott and Hort’s work, older manuscripts and fragments have been discovered that include the disputed words. The work of scholars like James Snapp has shown that the manuscript evidence is more complex than previously thought. The original argument that the oldest manuscripts are superior doesn’t hold water in this case.

### The Spiritual Stakes

This isn’t just an academic debate about manuscripts. This is about spiritual power—or the lack of it.

If the Enemy wanted to defang the Church, what better way than to convince believers that fasting is optional? That prayer alone is sufficient? That there’s no deeper level of spiritual warfare that requires sacrifice and discipline?

Refer back to Chapter 19. My wife and I prayed for her healing from Hepatitis C countless times over two years. We prayed earnestly. We prayed in faith. We prayed with others.

But it wasn’t until she **fasted** that she heard clearly from God and received the divine appointment that led to her miraculous healing.

Do you think we didn’t pray before that? Of course we did. But fasting was the breakthrough.

The same was true for my healing from degenerative disc disease. I had prayed for nearly a decade. But when I finally obeyed God’s call to fast, that’s when the demon was cast out and I was set free.

**This kind does not come out except by prayer and fasting.**

It’s interesting because during that decade of suffering, I eventually resolved that my answer to prayer must have been the same as Paul’s. That God’s grace was sufficient for me. Paul suffered with what he called a “thorn in his flesh,” but it was also revealed that the thorn in his flesh was actually a messenger of Satan sent by God to keep him humble. Why? Becuase of the exceeding revelation given to him. 

Paul’s physical suffering was the result of demonic oppression. Look again at how many times the Gospels interchangeably refer to sickness and demonic oppression. They are often synonyms. That doesn’t mean every disease is demonic, but it does show a compelling correlation between sickness and casting out demons for deliverance. This was also the case mentioned in Mark 9:29 and Matthew 17:21.

I’m not telling you to reject modern scholarship or throw away your Bible translation. I’m asking you to think critically about what’s been lost, and why.

When virtually every manuscript tradition includes these words, when the context demands them, when the biblical precedent supports them, and when our own experience confirms their truth, maybe we should reconsider whether “the oldest manuscripts” got this one right.

Or whether the thousands of witnesses across centuries were preserving something the Church desperately needs to recover.

**Fasting matters.**

In a detailed four-part examination of Mark 9:29, James Snapp, Jr. contends that the longer reading, “prayer and fasting,” is most likely original and that its omission in a small group of witnesses is secondary. He notes that over 99.9% of all extant Greek manuscripts and versional evidence for Mark 9:29 read “prayer and fasting,” whereas only three Greek manuscripts (Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus, and 0274), together with Codex Bobiensis (Old Latin k) and one early Georgian witness, support the shorter reading “prayer” alone. Snapp also points out that one oft-cited Greek witness for the shorter text, minuscule 2427 (“Ancient Mark”), has been demonstrated to be a modern forgery, and therefore cannot be counted as genuine evidence for the omission. In contrast, he argues that the early papyrus P45 most plausibly contained “and fasting” in Mark 9:29 on the basis of layout and spacing, making it an early Greek witness in favor of the longer reading. He further observes that in Codex Sinaiticus the main scribe wrote Mark 9:29 without “and fasting,” but a later corrector added the words, which implies awareness of a competing textual tradition that included fasting even within that Alexandrian milieu. Surveying early Christian literature, Snapp challenges the common claim that reverence for fasting would naturally lead scribes to add the phrase; he highlights evidence (for example, in the Apocalypse of Elijah) that some early Christians actually resisted or minimized fasting, which could instead motivate the removal or softening of fasting language. He then proposes plausible explanations for the omission: either a theological/apologetic tendency to avoid any suggestion that Jesus Himself needed to fast in order to exorcise certain demons, or a simple scribal error by homoeoteleuton, in which a copyist’s eye skipped from one “and” (καί) to the next, accidentally leaving out “and fasting.” On the basis of this combined external and internal evidence, Snapp concludes that the words “and fasting” were read widely across the early church, including in Egypt, and that the shorter reading adopted in many modern critical editions is best explained as a later aberration rather than the original text of Mark 9:29.
